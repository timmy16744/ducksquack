# A Day As A Platypus
_A young male, two seasons old, working a cold tannin-dark creek in the Tasmanian highlands. Wary, hungry, exact. He has never seen the thing humans call the sun directly — only the way it changes the water._

2. **The First Dive** — closing eyes and ears, the world becoming electric.

8. **Midday Drift** — the creek widens, slows, turns to glass and danger.

13. **The Long Burrow** — climbing back into the bank, pushing the water from his coat.

## 1. Before Light

I wake the way I always wake — not to light, because there is none down here, but
to cold. It seeps up through the packed earth of the sleeping chamber, through the
mat of grass and leaf I dragged in mouthful by mouthful, and it touches my belly,
and my belly tells the rest of me: the creek has dropped overnight, the creek has
gone colder, the creek is calling. I uncurl. My bill is the first of me to move,
sweeping the dark, and the dark is close and stale and mine, full of the smell of
my own fur and the river-mud I carry in on my feet. Above me the bank is a roof of
roots. I know every one of them by touch. I built this with my claws when the rains
came, two body-lengths of tunnel angled up so the water can never follow me in, and
a turning so tight that when I haul through it the walls press the wet from my coat
on both sides at once.

I am hungry. I am always hungry. This is not a complaint, it is simply the shape of
me — a small warm thing in cold water, burning myself to stay alive, needing to eat
a great share of my own weight before the next dark comes round. The hunger is a
clock. It is the only clock I have and it is never wrong.

I move down the tunnel toward the sound. There is always the sound, even here: the
creek's long muttering, the way it works at the bank, the way it carries every other
noise dissolved inside it. The entrance is below the waterline — I made it so on
purpose, so that nothing that walks can find the door. The last stretch of earth
turns to mud, and the mud turns to a coldness that is not earth at all, and then my
bill breaks through into the open water and the whole creek pours its information
over me at once.

I do not go up for air yet. I hang there a moment in the black, just under the
surface, and let the water tell me the night's news. It is colder than yesterday by
a little. It is moving faster — there was rain in the high country I will never
see, and the creek has risen and browned with it, thick with the tannin that comes
off the buttongrass and the rotting wood, the stain that makes this water the colour
of strong tea and turns the day, when it comes, into a dim amber nothing. Good. I
prefer it dim. I am built for dim, for the dark, for the in-between hours. The bright
flat middle of the day is when the shadows come, and the shadows are the only thing
in this world I truly fear.

I surface. It is the smallest part of my day, this — the rising, the quick working
of my lungs, the air going in cold and sharp. I do not love the air the way I love
the water. The air is where the danger lives. I take what I need and I look, because
I must, with eyes that are not much good in this light, and I see only the suggestion
of the bank, the blacker black of the overhanging fern, the faint paling far up that
might be the first thinning of the night. Then I tip forward, and I close my eyes,
and I close the soft folds over my ears, and I shut the whole bright frightening
world away — and I become what I am only ever fully able to be underwater. I dive.

And the moment my eyes close, the moment the water seals me in, the dark is not dark
anymore. It is lit. Not with light — I have no word for light, light means nothing
down here — but with the other thing, the thing only my bill can feel: the faint
electric flickering of every living body in the creek. My bill is not a mouth. My
bill is how I see. And the world it shows me is just beginning to wake.

## 2. The First Dive

Down. The cold closes over my back and I push into it with all four feet and the
broad rudder of my tail, and the creek-bed comes up to meet my bill out of a black
that is no longer empty. Everything alive is a small storm. I cannot tell you what
this is like in the way you would understand seeing, because it is not seeing — it
is closer to the way you might feel a held breath in a room, or know without looking
that someone has come up behind you. Every heartbeat in the water is a sparkle. Every
twitch of a muscle is a flash. The whole creek-bed crawls with faint electric life,
and laid over it, woven through it, is the other sense — the press of the water
against the thousands of tiny pits in my bill, the push and the pressure that tells
me where the bank is, where the stone is, where the current quickens around a snag I
cannot yet feel with my feet.

I sweep. This is the whole of my craft, the thing I have done since I first left my
mother's burrow, the thing I will do ten thousand times before this day is out. I
swing my bill side to side, side to side, low over the silt, and I read. A worm
turning in the mud is a slow dim pulse. A shrimp is quicker, brighter, a sharp little
star that winks out the instant it senses me and bolts — and the bolting is its
mistake, because the bolting is a flare, and I have already turned, already lunged,
already taken it into the soft pouches of my cheeks before my slow eyes would even
have known it was there. I do not chew. I have no teeth to chew with. I store the
catch in my cheeks and I grind it later, at the surface, between the horned pads
where teeth would be, mixed with a little grit I take up on purpose for the work.

The cold is the price of all of this. I feel it already, drawing the warmth out
through my belly where the fur is thinnest, and I know — the clock in me knows — that
I have perhaps a minute down here, maybe a little more, before my lungs and my cold
both call me up. So I do not waste a single sweep. I work a tight arc across the
gravel, taking two shrimp, a soft-bodied larva, something hard-shelled that I tuck
away to crack later. My feet stir the silt and the silt blooms behind me and it does
not matter at all, because I am not using my eyes, my eyes are shut, I gave them up
the moment I went under and I have not missed them once.

There — at the edge of my bill's reach, against the deeper hum of the channel, a
brighter knot of life. Several of them together, clustered in the lee of a stone.
Caddis, maybe, in their little stone houses, or a knot of worms the current has
gathered. I angle toward it, unhurried, because hurry is heat and heat is a thing I
cannot spare. The pressure against my bill swells as the stone nears. I read its
size, its lean, the slow eddy curling off its downstream face where the small things
shelter. I open my bill. I sweep it through them. And I have them, most of them, in
one pass — the cheek pouches fuller now, a small good weight, the hunger-clock easing
by the smallest degree.

My chest tightens. The cold has reached deeper than I like. Enough. I turn my bill
up toward the paler nothing overhead and I let my feet drive me back toward the air
I do not love, the catch heavy and live and shifting against my cheeks, the creek
still sparkling all around me with everything I did not take — everything I will come
back down for, again and again, until the dark comes round once more and the clock
in me finally, briefly, lets me rest.
