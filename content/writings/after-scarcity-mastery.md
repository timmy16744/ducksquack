title: "After Scarcity, Mastery"

I've been thinking lately about what comes after. Crises seem abundant enough and I don't mean after any of those. I mean after the long project of survival that has defined most of human existence. What do people do when they no longer have to do anything? The question used to feel like philosophy. It's starting to feel like something I need to answer for my son.

Let me explain what I mean, because I'm aware this sounds abstract in a way that risks losing the thread before it starts.

For most of human history nearly all human effort went towards meeting basic needs. Growing food. Building shelter. Defending against threats. The peasant farmer of medieval Europe never wondered about the meaning of work, because work was survival and survival wasn't optional. The question of purpose didn't arise because purpose was obvious. You worked or you died, your children worked or they died, and that was the human condition for ten thousand years.

What we call civilisation is, in one sense, the slow reduction of the effort required for survival. Each advance from agriculture to the steam engine to the microprocessor freed some portion of human capacity from the brute demands of existence, and the surplus made possible everything we value - art, science, philosophy, the long accumulation of knowledge separating us from ancestors who huddled in caves.

I find myself wondering whether we're approaching something like an endpoint of that trajectory. Not a utopia, since I'm suspicious of utopias, but a threshold past which the economics of survival change fundamentally enough that our questions about work and purpose and meaning need entirely different answers.

**The Curves**

The trajectory I observe suggests the cost of basic goods is falling towards zero, or close enough to zero to be functionally equivalent. That's a direction of travel which has held for decades and is now accelerating, rather than a prediction about next year.

Consider what artificial intelligence is doing to the cost of cognitive labour. Tasks that once required expensive professionals can increasingly be done by systems costing fractions of a cent per operation. Consider what automation is doing to manufacturing. Consider what solar is doing to the cost of power, which underlies almost everything else. The curves all point the same way, down and accelerating.

If they continue, and I see no physical or economic law that would stop them, we arrive somewhere unprecedented: a world where food, shelter, energy and transport become cheap enough that their cost no longer organises human life the way it always has.

I want to be careful, because predictions about technological transformation have a poor record. The flying cars never arrived. The paperless office generated more paper than ever. I hold my expectations loosely, aware the future has a habit of surprising anyone who thinks they can see it clearly.

The direction seems clear even where the timeline doesn't, and the direction raises questions I don't think we've adequately considered. Chief among them: what happens to meaning when survival stops being at stake?

**What I Know About This**

That's no idle question for me, and I've had some experience with it, though not by choice.

Cluster headaches forced me to construct a life that doesn't depend on conventional employment for its sense of purpose. When pain structures your existence to the degree mine is structured, you either find meaning outside the normal channels or you don't find it at all. I found it eventually - in my family, in work done for its own sake rather than for income, in the attempt to understand the world and leave something useful behind for my son.

That experience makes me both more optimistic and more cautious about the transition I'm describing. More optimistic because I know finding purpose without necessity is possible, having done it. More cautious because I know how difficult the finding is, how easily the thread is lost entirely, and how much the search depends on resources and support that not everyone has.

The pessimistic view holds that widespread freedom from necessity produces widespread purposelessness - that meaning has always been tied to struggle and without struggle there's none. The anomie of affluent societies and the mental health crisis among people who lack material want both suggest this is a genuine risk. We've already seen what happens when pockets of abundance emerge inside a broader context of scarcity, and the results aren't uniformly encouraging.

I don't think that captures the whole picture. What I observe, in myself and in others who've achieved some freedom from necessity, is the redirection of striving rather than its cessation. People don't stop wanting to create and build and solve and excel. They want these things more, if anything, because they can finally pursue them without the distortion survival pressure introduces.

**Mastery Culture**

I've come to think of the result as Mastery Culture. A condition in which work persists and its character transforms. People still create, still build, still pour themselves into projects demanding everything they have, and they do it because excellence is intrinsically meaningful rather than because survival requires it. The craftsman making furniture because beautiful furniture is worth making. The engineer designing systems because elegant systems are worth designing. The writer writing because the writing itself is the point.

I see glimpses already in the corners of the economy where survival pressure has receded. The open source developer contributing thousands of hours to projects that will never pay them. The hobbyist machinist whose tolerances exceed anything a commercial application requires. The musician spending years perfecting a piece a hundred people will hear. None of these are economically rational in the conventional sense. They're expressions of something deeper - the human drive towards mastery that emerges whenever circumstances permit it.

What strikes me is the intensity. The people I know who've found this kind of purpose don't work less than those driven by necessity. They frequently work more. The quality of the effort differs, though. There's a joy in it, an alignment between what they're doing and what they want to be doing, that survival labour rarely provides. The craftsman at midnight perfecting a joint nobody will ever see is flourishing in a way the wage worker watching the clock can't reach, whatever it looks like from outside.

The idea isn't new. The Greeks had a concept, arete, capturing something like it - excellence for its own sake, the full expression of human capability. Arete was available only to a small elite whose survival was guaranteed by slaves and servants. What I'm describing is the potential democratisation of that condition, extended to everyone, arriving through the collapse of the costs that always made survival the primary concern rather than through redistribution of existing wealth.

**Three Preconditions**

From here in Australia I find myself thinking about what this means concretely. We're twenty-six million people, resource-rich and manufacturing-poor, dependent on supply chains we don't control and security arrangements we didn't design. If the trajectory unfolds, our position changes fundamentally.

I write that with a particular awareness of the vulnerability. We export iron ore and coal and lithium and import nearly everything requiring sophisticated manufacturing. We can't make an EpiPen. We import ninety percent of our pharmaceuticals. When the pandemic disrupted supply chains we discovered how thin our self-sufficiency actually was, which is an uncomfortable position for a nation whose strategic environment grows more contested every year.

The same technologies creating that vulnerability might resolve it, and that's what I keep returning to.

Consider manufacturing. The economic logic of offshoring depended on labour cost differentials, and while production required human workers it made sense to locate factories where labour was cheap. As manufacturing automates, that logic weakens. Robots demand no wages and AI systems require no health insurance, so the cost advantages of distant production diminish while the risks of supply chain vulnerability remain exactly where they were.

What becomes possible is domestic production at competitive cost. Something new rather than a return to mid-century factories: automated facilities manufacturing sophisticated goods locally. A nation with that capacity becomes meaningfully self-sufficient, unable to be held hostage by distant suppliers, its resilience independent of the goodwill of trading partners or the stability of shipping routes.

I think of that as the Closed Loop - an economy that can provision itself without external dependency. Trade would remain valuable for variety and efficiency, so this is nothing like isolationism. It's sovereignty of a kind Australia has never held.

There's a parallel logic in defence. The traditional model centred on expensive platforms - carriers, fighter jets, systems costing billions and taking decades to develop - which are increasingly vulnerable to cheap countermeasures. A missile costing a few hundred thousand dollars can destroy a ship costing several billion, and the economics have inverted. What's emerging is autonomous systems costing thousands rather than millions, deployed in swarms that overwhelm through numbers. Companies like Anduril pioneered the approach, and the implications reach past military strategy. When defence becomes cheap and distributed, the calculus of aggression changes, and deterrence stops being a threat of retaliation and becomes a guarantee of failure.

I call that Peace Infrastructure - peace as a utility, as reliable and automatic as electricity or running water, rather than as the absence of war achieved through negotiation and maintained through vigilance. It sounds utopian and perhaps it is, and the technological foundations are becoming visible enough that I can't dismiss it.

There's a third element that may seem distant from manufacturing and defence, and it concerns money. The financial system rests on fractional reserve banking, where a deposit is mostly lent out and only a fraction held in reserve. That creates systemic fragility: if confidence fails and depositors demand their money at once, the system collapses. We've seen the pattern repeatedly and each time the response has been to paper over it.

For the transition I'm describing, that fragility is a serious obstacle. Innovation requires patient capital invested across long horizons, and the builders who would construct the infrastructure of abundance need to know their resources are secure. They can't operate if their capital is subject to periodic destruction. The alternative is simpler: banking where deposits are held in full, where the money you deposit is the money the bank holds. It sacrifices some credit expansion and provides absolute stability of capital. I think of it as Bedrock Capital.

Those three together - the Closed Loop, Peace Infrastructure, Bedrock Capital - constitute what I'd call the preconditions for Mastery Culture. Security that doesn't depend on the restraint of adversaries. Economic resilience that doesn't depend on distant supply chains. Financial stability that doesn't depend on confidence games.

I'm aware this sounds grandiose and that predictions of transformation have a poor record. I hold all of it loosely.

**Arthur**

I keep returning to my son. Arthur will grow up in whatever world we manage to build or fail to build, and if these trajectories continue he may live to see survival automated and the question of purpose requiring entirely new answers.

I want him ready for that. I want him to understand that meaning can be found in mastery and craft and the pursuit of excellence for its own sake, that the absence of necessity doesn't have to mean the absence of purpose, and that humans have always created and strived and reached beyond mere survival because the reaching is intrinsic to what we are rather than a response to scarcity.

The transition won't be seamless. There'll be disruption and displacement and the painful unravelling of arrangements that no longer serve. Some people will find the new world liberating and others will find it terrifying, and the psychological work of learning to draw meaning from excellence rather than necessity isn't trivial. Not everyone will manage it.

I think it's coming anyway, in some form, on some timeline. The alternative - that we continue indefinitely in the current mode with survival pressure organising human life as it always has - seems to me less likely than the transformation. The trajectories are too clear, the technologies too powerful, the direction too consistent.

After scarcity, mastery. After survival, craft. After necessity, the question of what we actually want to do with one precious life.

I don't know exactly what that world looks like and I doubt anyone can. I think about it more often as Arthur grows, watching him discover the satisfaction of making something with his hands, of solving a problem through persistence and attention. He knows nothing yet about economic necessity. For him the building is the point and the creation is the reward, and I want him to hold onto that even as the world teaches him work is something you endure for money.

Perhaps the world I'm describing would let him keep it. Perhaps all of us could recover something we lost when survival became the organising principle of existence. The child who builds for the joy of building is in touch with something fundamental that adults were forced to forget.

I want to be ready for what's coming and I want my son ready for it. The rustle in the grass carries more than a warning of danger. Sometimes it's the sound of something new approaching, and the wise, I suspect, are already listening.
