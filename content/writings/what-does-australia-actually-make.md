---
title: "What Does Australia Actually Make?"
date: "2025-06-18"
color: "green"
---

I've been running a small thought experiment lately. Imagine Australia were cut off from global supply chains tomorrow - not permanently, just for a year. What could we produce here? What would we lack? How long before the absences turned critical?

The answers aren't comforting.

We can't manufacture an EpiPen. We import roughly ninety percent of our pharmaceuticals, so if you or someone you love depends on a medication to stay alive, that medication almost certainly arrives on a container ship. The factories producing it, the supply chains delivering the precursor chemicals, the quality control infrastructure ensuring it's safe - none of that exists here at any meaningful scale.

We import eighty-five percent of our medical devices. The machines that diagnose disease, the implants that repair bodies, the monitors watching vital signs in intensive care: foreign, along with the expertise to build them and the components inside them.

We can't build a car. When Ford, Holden and Toyota left in 2017 they took an ecosystem with them, not just assembly lines. A hundred and twenty supplier companies closed or relocated, and the tooling and institutional knowledge and trained workforce dispersed. Deciding tomorrow that we needed domestic vehicles would mean starting from close to zero.

We have one oil refinery where we once had seven. Fuel security - keeping trucks moving and planes flying and farms operating - depends on refined petroleum arriving from overseas, and the strategic reserve would last weeks. In a serious disruption the country would start seizing up almost immediately.

We rank 102nd globally in economic complexity, between Bangladesh and Senegal. That isn't a typing error. The measure captures the sophistication and diversity of what a country produces, and ours has become, increasingly, digging things up and shipping them elsewhere.

Manufacturing has fallen from twenty-eight percent of GDP in the 1960s to under six percent, the lowest in the OECD. I want to be careful about how that gets explained, because the usual account treats it as a natural stage of economic development. Germany, Japan and South Korea have all maintained substantial manufacturing while achieving high living standards. They made choices. We made different ones.

**The Absurdity at the Centre**

So I locate the blame in policy rather than in any implacable logic of comparative advantage. Decades of it, bipartisan, consistent, and still running.

Consider the position we've engineered. We hold some of the world's largest coal reserves. We export that coal to China, which uses it to power factories that manufacture goods, which we then import at substantial markup. The energy that could have powered Australian manufacturing powers Chinese manufacturing instead, and we pay twice - once in the resource extracted, once in the finished product bought back.

The pattern repeats right across the economy. Iron ore leaves our ports and returns as steel. Lithium departs and comes back as batteries. Rare earths are extracted and shipped, then reimported inside electronics and motors and the ordinary components of modern life. We've made ourselves a mine with a housing bubble attached, and we did it deliberately, through settings that made the outcome inevitable.

The argument against manufacturing here has always been cost. Labour is expensive, energy is expensive, regulation is burdensome, and we can't compete with countries where workers earn a fraction of Australian wages and environmental standards go unenforced. Better to focus on what we do well and let others handle the making of things.

That argument had real force in an era of abundant globalisation and stable geopolitics. It has considerably less now. Supply chain disruptions over recent years exposed vulnerabilities economists had dismissed as theoretical. The strategic implications of depending on potentially hostile powers for critical goods have become impossible to wave away. And the assumption that cheap labour always outcompetes Australian production is being undermined by the same technology transforming everything else.

Automation changes the arithmetic. When manufacturing needs fewer workers, labour cost advantages shrink. When robots and AI systems can run a factory with minimal human intervention, the question shifts from wage differentials towards energy costs, regulatory environments and proximity to markets. On energy we have extraordinary potential. On regulation we have choices we haven't made. On proximity we sit inside the fastest-growing economic region on earth.

**What Could Be Built**

We could build automated battery factories. The lithium is here, demand is exploding, and large-scale production technology exists and improves yearly. No physical or economic law requires Australian lithium to be processed in China and returned as finished cells. Only policy settings that make that route more profitable.

We could build datacentres at scale, with land and renewable potential and political stability and rule of law. Global demand for compute is expanding faster than any other infrastructure category, and we could supply clean, reliable processing capacity for the whole Asia-Pacific. Instead we debate, study, commission reports, and watch the window narrow.

We could manufacture the components of the energy transition - solar panels, wind turbines, electrolysers for green hydrogen - where demand is effectively unlimited for decades. We have the raw materials, the energy to power production, and an educated workforce that could be trained for advanced manufacturing. What's missing is a policy framework that would make investing in any of it rational.

If I had to name one lever, it would be industrial electricity price. Everything else on that list is downstream of it. Automated manufacturing is, in economic terms, mostly a machine for converting electricity into goods, and a country with the cheapest industrial power on earth wins that conversion regardless of its wages. We have more sun and wind and uranium than almost anywhere, and among the highest industrial electricity costs in the developed world, which is one of the strangest sentences it's possible to write about a nation.

Fix that one number - through generation, transmission, and a genuine decision that industrial power is strategic infrastructure rather than a retail market - and the battery plants and datacentres and electrolyser factories become investable without anyone needing to subsidise them individually. Leave it where it is and every specific proposal requires its own grant, its own minister, and its own reason to be reversed by the next government.

I'm not arguing for autarky. Complete self-sufficiency is neither possible nor desirable, trade creates genuine value, and specialisation has real benefits. But there's a difference between strategic trade and strategic dependence, and we've drifted into the second while telling ourselves we were pursuing the first.

What Australia makes is finally a question about what kind of country we want. A quarry with good beaches, dependent on the continued goodwill of trading partners and the stability of supply chains we don't control? Or something more capable and more sovereign in the meaningful sense of the word?

I think about this when I consider what my son inherits. The financial circumstances matter and they're the smaller part. What I mean is the productive capacity of the country he'll live in. Will Australia be able to make the things that matter? Will we have the industrial base to respond to a crisis, to take an opportunity, to hold some autonomy in a world that may prove considerably less stable than the one I grew up in?

The resources are here and the potential is here. What's missing is the willingness to accept some cost today for capability tomorrow. Every year we delay, expertise atrophies, investment goes elsewhere, and the task gets harder. We could still build something, and the window is open for now.
