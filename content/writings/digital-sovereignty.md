There's a question I keep returning to. Where does Australia actually exist in digital terms? The landmass remains stubbornly physical and I don't mean that. I mean the infrastructure now underlying nearly every aspect of modern life. Where does it live, who controls it, and what does it mean that most Australians have never once considered the question?

Let me make it concrete. When an Australian business stores data in the cloud, that data almost certainly sits on servers owned by Amazon, Microsoft or Google - American companies, subject to American law, operating infrastructure that may be physically located here and is controlled from elsewhere. The data of Australian citizens and businesses and government agencies flows through systems we don't own and can't ultimately govern.

When Australians communicate, they do it mostly through platforms owned by foreign corporations. The messages, the metadata, the patterns of connection all pass through infrastructure we hold no sovereign claim over. The terms of service are written in California. Moderation decisions follow priorities that may or may not align with anything here. We're tenants in digital spaces owned by other people.

Our payment systems depend on foreign networks. Visa, Mastercard, the SWIFT system enabling international transfers - none of them Australian. If access were restricted, whether through a commercial decision or geopolitical pressure, the consequences would be immediate and severe. We've built an economy that can't function without infrastructure we don't control.

The telecommunications backbone is somewhat better and not as much better as we assume. The undersea cables connecting us to the global internet are critical vulnerabilities. There aren't many of them, they land at known locations, and they could in principle be severed or tapped or redirected. The operators are mostly foreign and the equipment is manufactured elsewhere.

I want to be careful here, because this argument has a paranoid version I don't hold. I'm not suggesting foreign technology companies are hostile or that their services should be refused. Global integration has brought enormous benefits, and the platforms I'm describing enable commerce and communication and innovation that would otherwise be impossible. The question was never whether to participate in global digital systems. It's whether to do it with our eyes open about what participation entails.

**Sovereignty With a Digital Dimension**

Sovereignty has traditionally meant control over territory. A nation is sovereign to the extent that it determines what happens inside its borders without external interference. That remains meaningful and it's become badly incomplete. In a world where critical infrastructure is digital, sovereignty has to include some degree of control over the digital systems national life depends on.

By that standard ours is considerably more limited than most citizens realise.

Consider a serious geopolitical crisis. A real deterioration in relations with a major power would do it; no war is required. Every dependency I've described becomes leverage. Cloud access could be restricted. Payment networks disrupted. Communications platforms throttled or monitored or simply switched off. We'd discover very quickly how much of what we take for granted rests on the continued goodwill of entities we don't control.

This isn't hypothetical. We've watched other countries discover these dependencies in real time and seen how fast digital infrastructure becomes a vector for coercion. Strategic autonomy requires some digital autonomy, and digital autonomy requires infrastructure that isn't wholly in someone else's hands.

The honest assessment is that the horse has largely bolted. The decisions that could have established meaningful digital sovereignty weren't made decades ago when they'd have mattered. We didn't develop domestic cloud infrastructure at scale, didn't build payment systems that could operate independently, and didn't create conditions for a technology industry capable of offering alternatives. We integrated enthusiastically without much consideration of what it would cost in autonomy.

Reversing that now would be extraordinarily difficult. Network effects entrench dominant platforms against any domestic challenger. The capital required for infrastructure at scale exceeds anything Australian investors have shown willingness to provide. Talent flows towards the established centres of the industry, which are not here.

**Where I'm Genuinely Uncertain**

So what do we do from where we actually are?

Complete digital sovereignty is neither achievable nor desirable, and I want to say that plainly rather than gesture at it. The costs would be prohibitive, the benefits of integration are real, and some interdependence is simply the price of participating in a modern economy. The goal should be resilience rather than autarky - the ability to absorb shocks, maintain essential functions when external systems fail, and hold options when a dependency turns into a liability.

That might mean keeping domestic capacity in critical areas even where foreign alternatives are cheaper, because redundancy has value that never appears on a balance sheet. It might mean data localisation for certain categories of sensitive information, so at least some Australian data stays under Australian jurisdiction. It might mean investment in domestic cloud infrastructure, aimed at ensuring an alternative exists at all rather than at replacing foreign providers.

I find myself genuinely uncertain where the line sits, and I'd rather admit that than manufacture confidence. I can see the case for accepting deep integration and managing the risk through diplomacy and diversification. I can equally see the case for pursuing domestic alternatives more aggressively at significant cost. National security concerns shade into protectionism easily. Sovereignty becomes an excuse for inefficiency easily. The boundary between prudent resilience and paranoid isolation isn't obvious, and I'm sceptical of anyone claiming certainty about it.

What I'm confident about is narrower. Critical government systems - health records, tax, welfare, defence administration, electoral infrastructure - should be hosted somewhere we can reach with our own law, and we should know, as a published fact, exactly where each of them currently sits.

I'd start there because it's the one part of this that requires no industrial policy, no capital we don't have, and no argument about protectionism. Hosting is a procurement decision that sits entirely inside our own gift. And I suspect the audit alone would be clarifying, because I don't believe anyone in Canberra could currently produce that list.

My son will inherit whatever arrangements we leave him. If they include deep vulnerabilities that could be exploited in a crisis, he'll carry the consequences of choices we made without fully understanding them. That's true of a lot we pass on, and digital infrastructure has a particular character: invisible until it fails, taken for granted until it's withdrawn. The dependencies accumulate silently, and by the time they're visible they may be very hard to unwind.

I'm not calling for panic. I'm calling for attention - for an honest assessment of where we stand, for recognition that sovereignty in this century has a digital dimension we've largely neglected, and for a conversation about what we're willing to pay in money and convenience for some autonomy over the systems modern life runs on.

The horse may have bolted. The stable still exists, and there will be other horses. What matters is whether we learn anything from how easily this one got out.
