---
title: "The Last Factory"
date: "2017-10-15"
color: "orange"
---

Ford closed first. Then Holden announced, then Toyota. By the time you read this the last car will have come off the last Australian production line, and an industry that once employed hundreds of thousands of people will exist only in the past tense.

I want to sit with what that means, because I don't think we've sat with it. The coverage has been economic - job losses tallied, retraining programmes announced, transition packages costed - and the language of closure has been managerial, as though a civilisational choice could be reduced to a spreadsheet.

It can't. What happened here is a national character event wearing the costume of an economic one. Australia decided, across several decades, to stop making things, and the decision is now complete.

**The Logic Was Sound**

Let me trace the reasoning, because it matters and because it was never stupid. That's precisely what made it dangerous.

The argument ran like this. Australia has high wages. High wages make manufacturing expensive. Countries with lower wages can produce the same goods more cheaply. Therefore we should focus on what we do well - mining, agriculture, services - and let others handle the making of things. Comparative advantage, Ricardo, the textbook case.

There's real truth in it. Australian wages are high, and a worker on an Adelaide assembly line earns many times what an equivalent worker earns in Thailand or Mexico. The cars we produced were neither cheaper nor better than the ones produced elsewhere, and subsidies kept the industry alive long past the point where it could survive on its own economics. So the subsidies were withdrawn, the industry collapsed, and we told ourselves this was the market working as it should.

Here's what the textbook leaves out. An automotive industry is an ecosystem rather than a collection of car factories. Ford, Holden and Toyota were the visible canopy. Beneath them sat a hundred and twenty supplier companies - component manufacturers, toolmakers, specialists in metallurgy and plastics and precision engineering, each employing people who knew how to do things most Australians have never thought about.

When the canopy falls the understory dies, and the comparison is literal. Those hundred and twenty companies didn't transition to other industries. They closed. The toolmakers didn't become app developers, they became redundant. The precision engineers didn't retrain as baristas, they dispersed, taking with them knowledge that existed nowhere else in this country.

Knowledge is the part nobody discusses. The economic analysis counts jobs and dollars, and it doesn't count the accumulated understanding of how to make things at scale - how to hold tolerances, how to manage a supply chain that delivers the right part to the right place ten thousand times without failure. That knowledge can't be written down or stored in a library. It's tacit and embodied and distributed across networks of people who've worked together for decades, and when those networks dissolve it dissolves with them. You can't reassemble it by posting a job advertisement or recreate it with a grant.

**Countries That Chose Otherwise**

Other nations faced identical pressures and chose differently, which is the part of this I find hardest to forgive.

Germany has high wages, higher than ours in many sectors, along with stringent environmental regulation, powerful unions and an expensive welfare state. By every measure the Australian policy consensus uses to explain why manufacturing is impossible here, it should be impossible in Germany. Germany manufactures enormously - sophisticated products, exported globally, commanding premium prices, with the automotive industry alone employing nearly eight hundred thousand people directly. They didn't abandon manufacturing when labour got expensive. They moved up the value chain, investing in automation and precision and the engineering culture that makes a Mercedes something a Camry is not.

Japan has almost no natural resources and imports virtually everything it needs to make things, and it remains one of the most formidable manufacturing nations on earth. The difference between Japan and Australia has nothing to do with resources, since we have resources they can only dream of. The difference is a cultural commitment to making things well, embedded in institutions and policy settings that have supported it across generations.

South Korea was within living memory among the poorest countries in Asia and now produces automobiles, semiconductors, ships and consumer electronics competing with the best anywhere. That happened because their government decided, as national strategy, that manufacturing capability was worth building and protecting even when the short-term economics argued otherwise.

We decided the opposite. That digging things up and shipping them elsewhere was sufficient, that services would fill the gap, that comparative advantage was a national strategy rather than an economic theory.

I want to be precise about responsibility. None of this was inevitable and none of it was the market operating under natural law. It was policy - decades of it, bipartisan and consistent, made by people in offices through the instruments of government over thirty years. At every point alternatives were available. At every point, examples of different approaches existed in countries with similar or higher cost structures. We chose this.

**The Mentality of Consumers**

What bothers me most is the lack of seriousness with which the choice was made.

I've travelled enough to recognise the difference between a country that takes its productive capacity seriously and one that doesn't. In Japan, manufacturing is a source of national pride. The factory floor is respected and the craftsman is honoured, and the ability to make things is understood culturally as essential to identity and security.

Here manufacturing is an afterthought. A legacy sector, something other countries do in places we'd rather not visit, producing things we'd rather not think about until they appear on a shelf. We have the mentality of consumers. We dig things up, ship them away, and buy back the finished products at a markup that would be comical if it weren't so consequential. Iron ore leaves our ports and returns as steel. Coal leaves and returns as electricity embodied in the goods we import. Lithium departs and comes back as batteries.

The argument that none of this matters - that in a globalised world it's rational to specialise and trade - rests on assumptions that are starting to look fragile. It assumes supply chains stay open, trading partners stay friendly, the global order stays stable, and there will always be someone willing to make things for us. Those assumptions have held for decades and may hold for decades more. They remain assumptions rather than laws, and a country that has dismantled its capacity to manufacture has also dismantled its capacity to respond if they fail.

So here's the practical question that deserves more attention than it gets. What does Australia actually make? Not what do we export, since we export rocks and crops and education and we're very good at it. What finished goods does this country produce? If the ships stopped arriving tomorrow, what could we provide for ourselves?

The answer is uncomfortable. We make remarkably little. We import most of our pharmaceuticals, most of our medical devices, most of our electronics, most of our vehicles, and most of the components of the infrastructure we depend on daily. We're a wealthy country dependent on others for the physical stuff of civilisation.

If I could preserve one thing from what's being lost, it wouldn't be an assembly line. It would be the toolmakers. A country that retains the ability to make the machines that make things retains the seed of everything else, and toolmaking is the deepest and least visible layer in the whole stack. A targeted apprenticeship and retention programme for the precision trades would cost a fraction of any single closure package and would keep the capability alive in a form that could be rebuilt from later.

I'd argue for it now, in the weeks around this closure, because the people are still here. In five years they'll be retired or dispersed or driving Ubers, and rebuilding from nothing takes a generation rather than a budget cycle.

The last car factory is closing, and it won't be the last closure. It's the most visible one, the one that makes the news, while erosion continues in sectors that attract less attention - textiles, chemicals, plastics, steel production shrinking, processing capacity declining - each treated as an isolated commercial decision rather than another point in a pattern.

I don't know what happens next. Perhaps services prove sufficient. Perhaps automation creates new forms of productive capacity that make traditional manufacturing beside the point. Perhaps the global order stays stable enough that our dependence never becomes a problem.

Perhaps. I keep thinking about those hundred and twenty supplier companies. The toolmakers. The precision engineers. The people who knew how to make things and no longer have anywhere to use it. They're dispersing now, into other jobs or into retirement, and what they knew is dispersing with them. We chose this, and we should at least be honest enough to look at what we chose while the people who could tell us are still available to ask.
