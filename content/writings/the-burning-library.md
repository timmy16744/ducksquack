---
title: "The Burning Library"
date: "2020-10-10"
color: "orange"
---

A man I met last year at a manufacturing conference told me he was one of the last people in Australia who could operate a particular type of extrusion machine. He was fifty-seven. His company had closed two years earlier and the machine, running since the 1980s, had been scrapped. Nobody had written down how to run it, because nobody imagined the writing down would ever be necessary. The knowledge lived in his hands and his head, and when he retired it would stop existing.

I've been thinking about him constantly since the pandemic started accelerating closures across manufacturing. Not because the closures are surprising - the decline has been running for decades - but because the pandemic has compressed what might have been a gradual process into a fast one, and the speed changes what happens.

When a factory closes slowly there's at least the possibility of transfer. A veteran might train a replacement. A company might document its processes. The institutional memory might find somewhere new to live, even in degraded form. When a factory closes suddenly - when supply chains break, when government support runs out, when marginal economics become definitively unviable - the knowledge simply goes.

**Every Factory Is a Library**

I want to argue that this is the least discussed and most consequential part of industrial decline. Not the jobs, though the jobs matter enormously. Not the output, though the output matters. The knowledge - the accumulated, embodied, distributed understanding of how to make things, which exists in no textbook and no database, only in networks of people who have done it together for years.

Every factory is a library, and I don't mean that in the inspirational-poster sense. I mean it literally contains knowledge that exists nowhere else. The machinist who can feel a cutting tool about to fail from the vibration coming up through the workpiece is holding information no sensor has been built to capture. The process engineer who knows a particular polymer behaves differently on humid days is carrying a data point that appears on no spec sheet anywhere. The production manager who knows which suppliers actually deliver and which will promise and fail is maintaining a map that exists in no system.

This knowledge is tacit and it resists being written down. It's acquired over years of practice, refined through thousands of small decisions, and embedded in the muscle memory and intuitive judgement of people who've done the work long enough to know what it actually requires rather than what the manual claims. We've spent a century getting extremely good at storing explicit knowledge and we've made almost no progress at all on storing this kind, which is why it keeps disappearing while we congratulate ourselves on the cloud.

When those people leave, it goes with them. It doesn't sit in a recoverable archive somewhere waiting to be found. It disperses into the general population where it degrades quickly through disuse, and inside a generation it's gone completely.

Australia has been burning its industrial libraries for decades. The auto industry took a hundred and twenty supplier companies with it in 2017, and every one of those was a library. The textile industry that contracted through the eighties and nineties was a library. The refineries that have closed, the steel mills that have shrunk, the chemical plants shut down - each one a library, burning, reported as a jobs number.

**We Have No Catalogue**

What worries me isn't that this is happening. Industrial change has always involved losing some knowledge and that's survivable. What worries me is that we have no framework for measuring what's going.

We track job losses. We track economic output, trade balances, GDP contributions. We don't track knowledge loss, because there's no metric for it, because tacit knowledge is by definition the kind of thing that resists measurement. So the single most consequential loss is the only one that appears in no account anywhere.

There's a related assumption underneath most of our policy response that I think is simply wrong. The reskilling story - that displaced workers move into the new economy - depends on the lost knowledge having been generic and transferable. It wasn't. A fifty-seven-year-old extrusion specialist doesn't become a data scientist, and not because he lacks intelligence. The knowledge he holds is specific, embodied and non-transferable. It's a different kind of knowing, and our economy doesn't currently price it, which isn't the same as it being worthless. It means we haven't yet found out what losing it costs.

The one thing I'd do, before any subsidy or industry policy or grand strategy, is start cataloguing. Not preserving everything, which is impossible, and not propping up plants that can't survive. Simply recording, systematically, while the people are still alive: which capabilities exist in this country, who holds them, how old they are, and what would be required to reconstitute each one from nothing.

It sounds absurdly modest against the scale of the problem, and that's rather the point. We are currently losing this knowledge without any record that it existed, which means we can't even have the argument about which parts are worth keeping. A catalogue would cost less than a single road interchange and would give us the one thing we completely lack: the ability to notice.

The cost will emerge eventually. It always does. When we need to build something and find nobody knows how. When a supply chain fails and there's no domestic alternative, because the people who could have provided one are retired or dead or driving Ubers. When a crisis demands manufacturing capacity that doesn't exist and can't be recreated on the timeline the crisis allows.

Civilisations decline when they lose knowledge. Losing wealth is survivable, and wealth can be regenerated in a decade. Knowledge, once gone, takes generations to rebuild if it can be rebuilt at all.

The libraries are burning, and we haven't started cataloguing. The man with the extrusion machine is sixty now. Somebody could still ring him.
