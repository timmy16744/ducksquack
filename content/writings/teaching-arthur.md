I've been thinking about curriculum lately. I don't mean the formal kind, with its syllabuses and standards and learning outcomes. I mean something underneath all of that. What am I actually trying to teach my son? If I had to write it down, what would be on the list?

The question has urgency because the world Arthur inherits is genuinely uncertain. The skills that served previous generations may prove worthless. The knowledge that guaranteed security may be obsolete. I can't predict what he'll need to know, and I can try to identify what stays valuable regardless of how things go.

I start with the practical, because it's the part I'm most confident about.

Cooking, as a basic competence for being alive rather than a hobby or a refinement. The ability to take raw ingredients and turn them into something nourishing. It seems almost too simple to write down, and I'm continually surprised by how many people reach adulthood without it. When you can feed yourself you have a foundation. When you can't, you're dependent in a way that constrains everything else.

Fixing things. When something breaks, the instinct should be to understand why and attempt a repair before reaching for a replacement or a professional. That's partly economic - the person who maintains their own possessions spends less - and it's more than economic. It's a disposition towards the world. Things aren't magic. They're systems with components and logic, that logic can be understood, and understanding it gives you agency.

I want Arthur to look at a broken appliance or a failed piece of furniture and see a puzzle rather than an obstacle. The puzzle may defeat him and some things are genuinely beyond amateur repair. The attempt is what matters. The willingness to open the case, examine the mechanism, form a hypothesis and test it - those transfer, and they apply to problems far beyond the physical.

Building, too. Basic carpentry, basic plumbing, basic electrical work. Tradespeople are enormously necessary and that has nothing to do with it. The person who holds those competences has options the person without them simply doesn't. Self-reliance was never about doing everything yourself. It's about being able to when circumstances require it.

These share a common thread, which is reducing dependence. Someone who can cook and fix and build is less vulnerable to disruption, and in a world that may become considerably more disrupted than the one I grew up in, that seems worth cultivating deliberately.

**The Part That's Harder**

Practical skills are the beginning. There's something harder to articulate that matters more.

Critical thinking, though the phrase has been worn almost meaningless through overuse, so let me be specific. I want Arthur to develop the habit of asking how people know what they claim to know. I mean genuine inquiry here, rather than a posture of scepticism, which is its own kind of laziness. What's the evidence? What are the assumptions? What would change my mind? Who benefits from me believing this?

You can't teach that directly. Nobody has ever been lectured into critical thinking. What you can do is model it - think out loud in their presence, show them what it looks like to meet a claim and examine it and reach a tentative conclusion while staying open to revision. Admit when you were wrong and explain how you found the error. Children learn what they see far more than what they're told, which is the reassuring and terrifying fact at the centre of all of this.

Trusting your gut, which might look like a contradiction of the above and isn't. Intuition is reason operating below the level of conscious articulation. When something feels wrong, the feeling usually contains information that hasn't been processed into explicit form yet. Learning to notice it, take it seriously, and not be enslaved by it is a genuine skill, and it can be the difference between walking into trouble and walking away.

The power of a smile. This sounds trite and it isn't. A genuine smile disarms people and opens doors that stay closed to neutral or hostile expressions. It costs nothing and returns a great deal. I've watched people move through the world with warmth and others move through it with coldness, and the warm ones have an easier time. The world isn't remotely fair and that has nothing to do with it. Human beings respond to human beings, and a smile is a signal of safety.

Being friendly and kind to everyone, as a default setting. I mean a starting position of goodwill, which is a long way from naivety and from assuming that everyone has earned trust. Most people in my experience answer kindness with kindness. The world gets marginally better when you treat strangers as potential friends rather than potential threats, and even where it isn't returned you've lost nothing by offering it.

**Where It Gets Complicated**

Here's where it becomes more difficult. Kindness without boundaries stops being virtue and becomes vulnerability.

I want Arthur to understand that being peaceful requires knowing when to be angry, and being kind requires knowing when to be hard. That sounds contradictory and I don't think it is.

The person who cannot be dangerous is harmless, which is a different quality altogether from peaceful. Peace is a choice made by someone capable of violence who chooses otherwise, and kindness is a choice made by someone capable of cruelty who chooses otherwise. The capacity is what makes it a choice. Without it you aren't choosing virtue, you're simply incapable of vice.

I'm kind through and through. I believe in forgiveness, in assuming good intent, in laughing off accidental slights. If someone harms me without meaning to I hold nothing against them, because life is far too short for grudges over accidents.

If someone harms me deliberately - if they act with malice, choosing to cause damage knowing exactly what they're doing - then I have no difficulty at all standing my ground and being as hard as the situation requires. That is the precondition for the kindness rather than a contradiction of it. People don't take advantage of you when they know limits exist, and they respect boundaries only where they believe boundaries will be enforced.

So I want him gentle without being weak, open without being naive, and aware that anger isn't the opposite of love but is sometimes its expression. You fight for what you care about. Never fighting may simply mean you never cared enough.

**The Hardest Thing to Say**

There's one more, and it's the one I find most difficult to convey.

I live with cluster headaches. The literature describes them as among the most painful conditions known. More than half my existence involves managing that pain, frequently alone and frequently without recourse. I didn't choose it. I chose only to keep going anyway.

I want Arthur to know that I try not to let it define me. The pain is real and the limitations are real, and so is the life built around them - the joy I find in him, in his mother, in the work I do and the things I think about.

The lesson I hope he takes from watching is simple enough: you're stronger than you believe. At nearly every point, in nearly every dimension, you have more capacity than you think you do. The limits you assume are often not limits at all. They're fears wearing the clothes of facts, and when you push against them they frequently give way.

I don't know what Arthur will face and I can't prepare him for specifics I can't foresee. I can prepare him for the general case: that life will be hard, that suffering is unavoidable, and that the question was never whether you'll struggle but how you carry yourself while struggling.

Values are caught more than taught. He'll learn from watching his mother and me far more than from anything we say, which is reassuring because I believe our values are good and terrifying because every failure and lapse and moment when we fall short of who we mean to be gets absorbed just as reliably.

So the curriculum I'm describing is a life rather than a lesson plan, and the teaching happens in the living of it. All I can do is try to live in a way worth copying, and hope that when he's old enough to choose his own path he'll have seen enough to choose well.

That's the task. I'm not certain I'm equal to it, and I'm entirely certain it's the most important thing I'll ever do.
