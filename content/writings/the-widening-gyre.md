I want to begin with something I believe deeply, which is that these tools should be available to everyone. Anyone willing to engage seriously with artificial intelligence ought to be able to learn it, benefit from it, and fold it into their work and their thinking. That conviction matters to me, and it's the reason I find the present moment so difficult.

Because what I'm observing has started to erode my optimism, and I'd rather say so plainly than pretend otherwise.

I've been tracking adoption patterns fairly carefully, and the distance between insiders and outsiders has become extraordinary. Possibly unprecedented. In certain circles people have genuinely restructured their lives around orchestrated networks of agents, consulting these systems before nearly every decision, at a degree of cognitive integration that until recently belonged to speculative fiction of the non-utopian variety.

Then there's everywhere else. Knowledge workers petitioning for permission to use a basic assistant inside their workplace software. Entire organisations where AI remains an abstraction - something other people are doing, somewhere else, in a future that may or may not arrive.

I want to be fair to the institutions imposing those restrictions, because they aren't behaving irrationally. They face real concerns about security, compliance, liability and data governance, and the locally sensible choice is very often caution. The tragedy here is structural. Organisations making individually defensible decisions are collectively producing an outcome that damages the very people those decisions were meant to protect.

**What's Actually Being Learned**

One could argue that early adopter communities have always felt this intense from the inside, and that's fair. I'm less certain of it than I used to be. What I suspect is that we're watching a cultural phase transition running in parallel with the technical one, and that these are separate phenomena which both deserve attention.

Let me be precise about what's being acquired inside the bubble, because there's a tendency to trivialise it. When people describe AI fluency as learning to use ChatGPT, they misunderstand the shape of the skill entirely.

What's developing is tacit knowledge, and tacit knowledge resists explicit articulation by definition. It's the intuition for what a model can and can't do. The craft of building a prompt that produces genuine insight instead of plausible noise. The judgement to know when an output can be trusted and when it needs probing. The ingrained habit of reaching for these systems first rather than last.

None of that is a discrete skill you pick up in an afternoon tutorial. It's closer to a disposition, a way of thinking, and it compounds over months of daily practice. The people inside the bubble have been accumulating it for years - building mental models, developing workflows, making thousands of small errors and corrections that gradually sharpened their instincts. And it's difficult to transfer by its nature. You can explain prompting techniques and share workflows readily enough. The underlying intuition requires immersion, and I know of no shortcut.

**The Gap Isn't Stable**

Here's what concerns me most, and it seems obvious once stated while its implications are severe. People who use these systems fluently become better at using them. Their skills compound. They build workflows on top of workflows, integrate tools into tools, and develop instincts that let them extract more from every new capability that appears. Each month of fluency creates fresh distance from everyone still waiting for approval.

At this point someone usually reaches for historical comfort. The printing press. The internet. The spreadsheet. Previous technological divides eventually narrowed, literacy became universal, digital natives emerged. I take that argument seriously and it deserves better than dismissal.

I'm just no longer sure the comparison holds. Previous technologies eventually stabilised, and the gap between an expert and a novice spreadsheet user, while real, became manageable. You could catch up, because the target held still long enough to be reached. This isn't holding still. Capabilities are improving faster than the teaching infrastructure can adapt, so that by the time we've built decent curricula for today's tools, tomorrow's have arrived with entirely new affordances. The early adopters ride the wave. Everyone else watches it recede.

We've already seen how path dependence works in this domain. The organisations that understood scaling laws early and accumulated compute before 2022 established advantages that turned out to be insurmountable in practice. The distance between frontier labs and everyone else widened as the technology matured. I find myself wondering whether the same thing is now happening at the level of individual people - whether we've crossed a threshold past which catching up becomes, if not impossible, then costly enough to amount to the same thing.

There may be reason for hope. Perhaps interfaces become so intuitive that today's fluency turns into a quaint artefact of the era when special knowledge was required. I want to believe that. I'm not yet persuaded by it.

**The Part That's Harder to Say**

I've been writing about access and institutions, and I'd be dishonest if I didn't acknowledge another dimension of this, one that's more uncomfortable to raise.

There is fear here. Genuine fear.

Knowledge workers whose expertise may be disrupted have emotional reasons to disengage - to minimise the significance of all this, to find grounds for believing it won't reach their domain, their work, their sense of who they are. That isn't stupidity or laziness. It's a deeply human response to a perceived threat, and I'd expect it of anyone including myself.

There's also simple overwhelm. The pace is genuinely disorienting. New models monthly, capabilities expanding in unpredictable directions, and for someone already stretched thin by the ordinary demands of their job, adding "learn to use AI" to the list can feel impossible. Easier to wait and hope the dust settles.

I have compassion for that, and I think those of us inside the bubble are obliged to. The alternative is a contempt that helps nobody and understands nothing.

But compassion doesn't resolve the underlying problem, which brings me to a question I can't answer. What do we owe? Those of us who've had the access and the inclination and the time to develop fluency - is there a duty to bridge this, to teach and share and argue for broader access? Or is that paternalistic, a sort of technological noblesse oblige assuming others need rescuing? I genuinely feel the pull of both positions.

What I keep returning to is that the institutional restrictions are the part we could actually change tomorrow. An organisation that permits its people to use these systems on non-sensitive work, with a clear list of what's out of bounds, converts a blanket prohibition into a bounded one, and the difference between those two is roughly the difference between a workforce that develops intuition and one that never gets the chance. It requires a policy document and costs nothing. And it's currently the single largest input into who ends up on which side of this.

I began by saying I believe these tools should be accessible to everyone, and I do believe that entirely. What I'm no longer sure about is whether the belief, however sincere, does anything at all on its own.
