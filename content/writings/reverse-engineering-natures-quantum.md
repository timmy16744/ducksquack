---
title: "Reverse Engineering Nature's Quantum"
date: "2025-01-06"
color: "teal"
---

There's a strange symmetry running through the history of human discovery. For millennia electricity flowed through every atom, crackled across the sky, and pulsed through every thought in every human brain, and we stumbled around in the dark unable to use any of it. We could see it. We could be frightened of it. We simply didn't have the understanding to make it do anything.

I suspect we're living through the same moment with quantum phenomena, except that this time the stakes are considerably higher and the timeline is considerably shorter.

Consider what happens inside a leaf. Every photosynthetic organism on Earth is running what amounts to a quantum optimisation algorithm, solving a problem with near-perfect efficiency that would bring our largest supercomputers to a halt. That isn't a metaphor - plants genuinely exploit quantum coherence to move energy through their molecular structures, and they've been doing it for billions of years. Birds navigate using quantum effects in their neurons. Bacteria tunnel through energy barriers that classical physics says should stop them.

We talk about building quantum computers as though we're creating something new. What we're really doing is catching up to something nature worked out before there were eyes to see it with. The trees outside your window have been computing since long before anything existed that could be impressed by it.

Progress here over the past few years has been genuinely remarkable. Investment is roughly doubling each year. Companies that treated this as a distant curiosity are building commercial applications. The question has quietly shifted from whether the technology will transform our world to which industries go first.

The timeline that looks increasingly plausible runs something like this. Within a few years, error-corrected systems reach practical reliability. By the end of the decade we begin designing materials atom by atom and synthesising pharmaceutical compounds in days rather than years. Beyond that, capabilities that sound fantastical from here - precise intervention in weather systems, perhaps forms of consciousness backup.

I know how that reads, and scepticism is the right response. I'd only note that these aren't drawn from science fiction. They're extrapolations made by researchers currently working at the frontier, and the history of this particular field is that the conservative estimates have been wrong more often than the wild ones.

The thought that strikes me hardest is a different one. What if we're surrounded by computation we simply don't recognise as computation? What if the biosphere is already, in some meaningful sense, a quantum computer - and a far more sophisticated one than anything we've assembled?

The implications are difficult to hold in your head. Every forest, every field, every organism, running quantum processes as an ordinary byproduct of being alive. The capacity is already here. It has always been here. What's missing is the interface.

I find myself imagining a future where we learn to connect to that existing infrastructure - where forests produce timber and solve logistics problems at the same time, where agricultural systems optimise their own growth while modelling traffic for the city down the road. It sounds fanciful. So did the idea of a global information network two centuries ago, and that one turned out to be an understatement.

The promise is genuinely breathtaking. Cancer treatments targeting disease at molecular precision. Ageing understood and possibly reversed through quantum-optimised genetic repair. Materials stronger than diamond and lighter than foam. Climate models accurate enough to let us intervene before a disaster rather than after it.

The terror is just as real, and I don't think we should talk about one without the other. Every economic model we have assumes constraints this technology may dissolve. Entire industries could face obsolescence in a compressed timeframe that defeats adaptation entirely. The psychological adjustment required is hard to overstate - imagine explaining a smartphone to someone from 1850, then multiply the disorientation by an order of magnitude and remove the two centuries they had to get used to it.

When we reach systems with thousands of error-corrected qubits, which current progress suggests could happen inside this decade, we won't merely have better computers. We'll have something closer to reality engines, able to simulate physical systems with a fidelity that makes the distinction between modelled and real genuinely hard to defend.

Nobody knows how quickly any of this arrives, and the experts disagree with each other by a decade or more in either direction. But we're approaching it with the same casual confidence we brought to social media, which was just a way to keep up with friends, and to artificial intelligence, which was just a tool for playing chess. We have a reasonable track record at building these things and a terrible one at anticipating what they do to us once they exist.

Every transformative technology has a last normal day. The last day before the internet connected every mind. The last day before smartphones put a supercomputer in every pocket. Nobody marked either one on a calendar.

My children will grow up in a world where matter is programmable and the boundary between physical and digital has stopped meaning very much. They'll look at our silicon chips and binary processors the way we look at slide rules and telegraphs - as clever work by people doing their best without the right tools.

Nature has been running these algorithms for billions of years, waiting for something to learn the language. The trees are already computing. The bacteria in your body are already processing. The network has been operational since roughly the beginning, and we are only now, blinking and unprepared, finding our way to the login screen.
