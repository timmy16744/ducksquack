---
title: "Parenthood in the Age of Super Intelligence"
date: "2025-04-20"
color: "pink"
---

My son was born into the strangest few years our species has ever lived through, and he has no idea. He is small and warm and entirely occupied with the problem of his own hands. Somewhere above his head, the thing I've spent years reading about is arriving, and when he's old enough to ask me what it was like, I want to have an honest answer ready.

Before Arthur, I thought I understood what it meant to care about something. I was wrong in a way that's difficult to convey to anyone who hasn't been through it. If I had to put it simply, I operated on a scale of one to ten before him, and afterwards I discovered the scale goes to a thousand. His first breath brought a sense of responsibility that wasn't only about feeding and sleep, though there is plenty of that. It was about the world he'd arrived into, which is stranger and less settled than the one any previous generation has handed over.

I've always had a deep conviction that human ingenuity is equal to whatever it creates. I still believe that. But conviction and certainty aren't the same thing, and I've noticed that becoming a father has made me far less willing to accept a comfortable answer to a hard question.

**What He's Actually Inheriting**

Arthur's early years coincide with what I expect historians will treat as the hinge of the century - the arrival of systems that reason, create and adapt in ways that increasingly resemble human cognition. Not the narrow, task-specific tools that have existed for decades. Something different in kind.

I find myself caught between wonder and concern, and I've come to think that's simply the correct place to stand rather than a failure to make up my mind. The wonder is easy to justify. Problems that have resisted generations of effort may yield inside his childhood - from accelerating drug discovery to designing new materials to understanding the biology we're built from. He may live in a world where the limiting factor on human progress isn't resources at all.

The concern is just as easy to justify. Our educational systems, economic structures and social arrangements were all designed for a world that's ceasing to exist. The skills that guaranteed security for my generation may be worth nothing to his. The institutions that provided stability may not survive intact, and I have no way of knowing which ones will go.

I think about education constantly, because it's the part I can actually touch. The system that shaped me rewarded standardised outcomes, rigid structures and skills that are now being automated at speed. Memorisation, calculation, routine analysis - these were valuable and they're becoming commodities. What isn't yet automatable are the things we've never systematically taught: creativity that comes from genuine understanding rather than pattern matching, judgement that only forms by wrestling with real uncertainty, and the emotional intelligence that arises from having actually lived.

There's a paradox here I keep circling. In teaching machines to think, we're being forced to work out what thinking is. In building systems that process information with superhuman efficiency, we're discovering that information processing was never the point. We've spent a century measuring children on precisely the dimension that has just become free, and we're going to have to stop.

**What I'm Actually Doing About It**

The honest answer is that I'm not preparing him for a job, because I don't know what jobs there will be, and I've stopped pretending the question has an answer. What I'm trying to build in him instead is the ability to want something specifically. These systems will do very nearly anything that can be clearly asked for, which makes the asking the entire game. A person who knows precisely what they want to exist has enormous leverage now. A person waiting to be told what to work on has almost none. Most of what I do with Arthur, underneath the blocks and the noise, is practice at wanting things on purpose.

The rest is smaller and older. I want him to build things rather than only consume them, so that making something feels ordinary to him rather than special. I want him to ask how anyone knows what they claim to know, including when the source is a machine that sounds certain and including when it's me. I want him bored often enough to develop an interior, because every system he'll encounter is optimised to prevent exactly that, and the capacity to sit with an unresolved thought may turn out to be the rarest thing he owns.

None of this is a defensive strategy against displacement, though it may serve as one. I want these things for him because they're the best of what a person can be, and they'd have been worth wanting in any century.

**The Questions I Can't Answer**

The economics trouble me and I don't want to write past them. When machines can produce material abundance, the distribution of that abundance becomes the central political question of his adult life. I don't know what form the answer takes. Perhaps some version of a universal income, perhaps something nobody has thought of yet.

And underneath the economics are questions that technologists have no special standing to answer. What will meaning and purpose be, in a world where contribution doesn't require scarcity? What values do we want to live by when the constraints that shaped our values have gone? What happens to the human condition itself when the thing that distinguished us stops being ours alone? These can't be settled by the people building the systems, and I notice I'd rather Arthur's generation answered them than mine.

I do have one clear view about the shape of it. The capability that's arriving should be widely held rather than owned by a handful of institutions, and the reason is simple enough: the answer I'd want for my son is the same answer I'd want for everyone else's children. A world where every child has these tools is a world where whatever advantages Arthur ends up with are ones he actually earned.

**Not Yet Written**

Becoming a father at this particular moment feels less like a personal event than a responsibility I've been handed. The decisions made in the next few years will run through his entire life, and he'll be living with the consequences long after I'm gone.

I can't control any of it and I've given up predicting it, because every prediction I've made about this technology has been wrong about the timing and usually about the direction. What I can do is prepare him without knowing what he's being prepared for - by building the things that hold their value in any world: adaptability, curiosity, compassion, and the capacity to find meaning in a life that won't stop changing underneath him.

He's asleep as I write this, with no opinion whatsoever about the singularity. There's a window, right now, before any of this is settled, and what we do inside it will shape the world he wakes up into. That's a daunting thing to hold. I'm not certain I'm equal to it. I'm completely certain it's worth attempting.
