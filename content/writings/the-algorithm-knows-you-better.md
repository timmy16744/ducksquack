Something happened this year that deserves more attention than it received, and it received quite a lot.

Facebook's advertising platform now allows targeting at a level of psychological specificity that would have sounded like science fiction ten years ago. You can target people likely to be feeling insecure. People who've recently moved. People whose purchasing behaviour suggests a relationship has ended. People who are probably pregnant before they've told anyone. The platform knows because the data knows, because the patterns in human behaviour are legible to anyone with enough data and the computational power to read them.

I've spent enough time in product design to understand what this means technically. The models aren't magic. They're correlations - immensely sophisticated correlations, trained on the behavioural residue of two billion people. Every click, every pause, every scroll that lingers a moment too long is a data point, and in sufficient quantity data points become a portrait. An imperfect portrait, and a useful one. Useful enough to sell things. Useful enough to shift behaviour. Useful enough to matter.

What interests me is the asymmetry rather than the technology.

Two billion people are legible to a handful of platforms - their preferences, vulnerabilities, habits and fears all readable and all actionable. The platforms meanwhile are opaque. Opaque to the users feeding them. Opaque to the governments nominally regulating them. Increasingly opaque even to the engineers building them, as the models grow complex enough that their specific reasoning becomes hard to trace.

This is a new form of power and I want to be precise about the claim, because power is a word people throw around loosely. What I mean is that the ability to model human behaviour at scale is the ability to influence human behaviour at scale. The entities holding that ability aren't governments or elected bodies or institutions with any democratic accountability. They're corporations headquartered in California, answerable to shareholders, optimising for engagement.

The framing everyone uses is that the user isn't the customer, the user is the product. I think even that undersells it. The user is the raw material. The product is prediction - the ability to forecast what a specific person will do or want or feel or buy, accurately enough to be commercially valuable. The customer is whoever pays for access to that predictive power. Advertisers obviously, and the same infrastructure is available to anyone with a budget: political campaigns, foreign governments, intelligence agencies, anyone who benefits from knowing what people think and being able to shape what they think next.

**Australia's Particular Position**

I keep returning to what this means for a country like ours.

We have no domestic platforms of any significance. No Australian search engine, no Australian social network, no Australian cloud infrastructure at the scale that matters. The digital infrastructure through which Australian public life increasingly operates is entirely foreign-owned, mostly American, and subject to the laws and commercial incentives of a jurisdiction that isn't ours.

When Australians discuss politics on Facebook they do it inside a system whose algorithmic priorities are set in Menlo Park. When they search for information, the ranking is determined by engineers in Mountain View. When they store data in the cloud it sits on servers owned by Amazon or Microsoft, under American law, accessible through American legal frameworks Australian citizens have no way to influence.

We lack a good vocabulary for this kind of dependence. It isn't colonialism in the traditional sense - nobody is being coerced, the platforms are genuinely useful, and people choose them because they're good products that deliver real value. The dependence is voluntary, which makes it harder to name and much harder to resist.

But voluntary dependence on systems you don't control and can't influence is still dependence. And dependence on systems that can model your behaviour, anticipate your choices and shape your information environment is a particular kind that we should be considerably more concerned about than we are.

The regulatory response has been inadequate, and that isn't specifically an Australian failure. No government anywhere has worked out how to regulate these platforms effectively. The technology moves faster than the regulatory process. The platforms hold more technical expertise than the agencies overseeing them. The lobbying budgets are substantial and the revolving door turns smoothly.

Our situation has an additional dimension that makes it worse. We aren't merely failing to regulate effectively. We're attempting to regulate systems we have no domestic alternative to. When the European Union pushes back against American platforms it does so from some position of strength - a large market, some domestic capability, institutional frameworks with teeth. When we push back, we do it as a small market with no alternatives and very little leverage.

The Assistance and Access Act, passed this year, illustrates the confusion perfectly. The government wants access to encrypted communications for law enforcement, and set aside the merits of that goal entirely. The implementation requires Australian law to reach into systems designed, built and operated by foreign companies under foreign law. The assumption that this is possible - that our legislation can meaningfully compel behaviour from entities whose infrastructure and expertise and legal domicile are all somewhere else - reveals a gap between how the government thinks these systems work and how they actually work. We're legislating control over infrastructure we don't own, and the exercise amounts to theatre.

**What Can Still Be Done**

Whether a real solution remains available I honestly can't say. The decisions that could have established meaningful digital sovereignty weren't made decades ago when a domestic technology industry might have been cultivated. We integrated enthusiastically into global systems because they were good and free and nobody thought hard about the cost.

The measure I'd argue for now is narrow and achievable: legislate a right to your own behavioural profile. Any platform operating here should be required to show any Australian, on request and in plain language, what it has inferred about them - the categories it has placed them in, the predictions it holds, the segments it sells them within.

I'd start there rather than with content rules or competition law for a specific reason. The entire asymmetry rests on invisibility. People can't object to inferences they've never seen, can't correct them, and can't judge whether the trade they're making is one they'd accept. Almost nobody would exercise the right, and that isn't the point. The point is that a system which must be able to explain what it has concluded about a person behaves differently from one that never has to. We did this with credit files decades ago and the sky didn't fall.

The asymmetry is growing regardless. The platforms know more about Australians than any Australian institution does. They can model our behaviour, predict our choices, shape what we see. They do it out of commercial logic rather than malice, which is in some ways more concerning, because commercial logic doesn't stop when consequences become problematic. It optimises regardless.

The algorithm knows you better than you know yourself. That's the claim, and the evidence increasingly supports it. The question nobody in Canberra seems to be asking is what it means to be a democracy when the most powerful tools for understanding and influencing citizens sit with entities the democratic process cannot reach.

I don't have a complete answer. I think the question is worth stating clearly anyway, because the longer we avoid it the fewer options remain by the time we're forced to confront it.
