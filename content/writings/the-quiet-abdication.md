---
title: "The Quiet Abdication"
date: "2017-06-20"
color: "red"
---

I've been watching the American alliance from Adelaide with the particular attention of someone whose national security depends on it. It's the uncomfortable attention of a tenant who has noticed cracks in the ceiling and is trying to work out whether they're cosmetic or structural.

Six months into the Trump presidency, I think they're structural.

I want to be careful, because the discourse around Trump is polarised enough that any observation about American strategic direction gets sorted immediately into a partisan bin. Whether he's a good or bad president matters far less to me than what his election, and the doctrine emerging from it, reveals about the durability of the security architecture Australia has relied on for seventy years.

America First is a strategic reorientation wearing the clothes of a slogan. The idea that American alliances should be transactional - that the United States should weigh the cost and benefit of each commitment and withdraw from the ones that don't pay - isn't a Trumpian invention and has deep roots in American political culture. What's new is that it has never before been the explicit framework of a sitting administration.

NATO is wobbling. Not collapsing, wobbling. The language of unconditional commitment has been replaced by the language of burden-sharing, and the Article 5 guarantee has been publicly questioned by the American president. Questioned rather than repudiated, which in alliance politics amounts to something similar. An alliance whose guarantor expresses doubt about whether the guarantee is worth honouring has already changed, whether or not the guarantee is ever formally withdrawn.

From here this matters more than most Australians appreciate. Our entire strategic posture rests on the assumption that the United States will show up when it counts. ANZUS is the foundation. Pine Gap, the joint facilities, the interoperability of our forces with American forces - all of it rests on the premise that the alliance is durable, that American power projection into the Indo-Pacific is permanent, that we can trade freely with Asia under the protection of American naval dominance indefinitely.

We have no Plan B.

I find that extraordinary. A country of twenty-five million occupying an entire continent, surrounded by water, dependent on maritime trade for virtually everything, with no independent capacity to secure its own sea lanes and no serious contingency planning for a world where the American guarantee weakens.

**Countries That Take It Seriously**

I've spent time in places that treat their own security as their own problem, and the contrast is instructive.

Singapore, with a population smaller than Sydney, maintains a military capability that is relative to its size among the most formidable anywhere. Conscription is universal, defence spending is substantial, and the strategic culture is self-reliance tempered by alliance rather than dependence dressed as alliance.

Israel, surrounded by states that have at various points sought its destruction, has built an indigenous defence industry producing some of the most advanced military technology on earth. When Israel enters an alliance it does so as a partner with capability rather than a dependent seeking cover.

Switzerland hasn't fought a war in five hundred years and maintains universal military service and an arms industry that exports globally. The neutrality is active rather than passive, and it's backed by the capacity for self-defence.

Our approach differs. We've outsourced our security to the United States with a thoroughness that would be impressive if it weren't so reckless. We don't manufacture fighter aircraft. We don't build submarines in any meaningful sense, though we discuss it endlessly. Our intelligence capabilities are integrated with American systems deeply enough to make independent operation difficult. Our strategic planning treats American presence in the region as a given rather than a variable.

This has worked beautifully for seventy years. The umbrella held, the trade routes stayed open, the region remained by historical standards remarkably stable. We grew wealthy underneath it and invested the wealth in housing rather than capability.

**Fraying Rather Than Folding**

What I keep returning to is what happens when the umbrella frays. Not collapses - frays. The distinction matters enormously. A sudden American withdrawal from the region would be a crisis commanding immediate attention. A gradual reduction in commitment, a slow shift in priorities, a progressive narrowing of the circumstances in which the guarantee applies: that's much harder to see and much harder to respond to.

I think that's what we're watching. A qualification rather than an abdication. America First means America's interests first, and there's no guarantee those interests in the Indo-Pacific will always align with ours. The alliance has always contained an asymmetry - we need it more than they do - and the asymmetry was masked by the cold war, by shared values, by the habit of cooperation. Remove the cold war framework, add a president who thinks explicitly in transactional terms, and the asymmetry becomes visible to everyone including our neighbours.

What should we do? The honest answer is that our options are narrower than they should be, having been narrowed by decades of decisions.

We could invest seriously in independent defence capability, which would mean spending substantially more than we do now, sustained over decades, on things we currently obtain through the alliance. We could pursue strategic diversification - deeper security relationships with Japan, India, Indonesia, the European powers that retain regional interests - as a hedge rather than a replacement. We could invest in the forms of national resilience that make a country harder to coerce: domestic manufacturing, energy independence, food security, the ability to keep functioning at reduced capacity if external support is interrupted.

If I had to choose one, it would be the least glamorous of the three. Fuel, munitions and the industrial capacity to sustain a force for more than a few weeks. Not new platforms, which absorb decades and headlines and arrive obsolete. Stocks and sustainment, which absorb neither.

I'd choose it because sustainment is the thing an ally actually provides and the thing we've never once had to supply ourselves. Every procurement debate in this country concerns what we would fight with. Almost none concerns how long we could fight at all, and that second number is the one that determines whether a guarantee is something we rely on or something we merely hope for.

None of these options is being seriously pursued. The national conversation about defence stays in a comfortable loop - reaffirm the alliance, announce a procurement project, defer the difficult questions to the next electoral cycle.

Meanwhile the language in Washington changes. The commitments become conditional and the calculations become explicit, and in Canberra nobody seems to have noticed, or having noticed, nobody seems to know what to do.

This moment demands a particular quality of attention. Not panic, which is useless. Not denial, which is where we currently live. Something more like the focused assessment of a patient who's received a diagnosis that may or may not be serious. You don't ignore it and you don't catastrophise. You examine the evidence, consider the possibilities, and begin preparing for the outcomes you'd rather not face.

The alliance may endure for decades and strengthen under future administrations. The wobble may prove temporary and the cracks cosmetic. But a country that depends on an alliance for its survival and doesn't prepare for the possibility that it weakens has confused hope with strategy, and hope isn't a plan.

The abdication is quiet. It's happening in press conferences and policy documents and the careful language of diplomatic communiqués, and it isn't dramatic enough to lead the evening news here. It's happening all the same, and the people whose job it is to notice should be paying closer attention than I believe they are.

From Adelaide I watch, which is all I can do from here. I watch with a growing conviction that the world my generation inherited isn't the world that will persist, and that the sooner we say so out loud, the better our chances of handling what follows.
