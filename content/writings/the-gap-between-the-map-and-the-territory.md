---
title: "The Gap Between the Map and the Territory"
date: "2023-04-15"
color: "red"
---

I want to write about a particular kind of dishonesty. Not the kind where somebody says a thing they know to be false, which is at least legible and can be argued with. The kind where an entire society maintains, through institutional consensus, an official account that bears no relationship to what's actually happening.

Global carbon emissions reached a record in 2022. Forty billion tonnes, the highest ever recorded. That happened in a year when the Paris Agreement was in force, when net zero pledges had been announced by virtually every major economy, and when the language of climate action was more prominent in policy documents and corporate communications than at any point in history.

The map says we're reducing emissions. The territory says we're increasing them. The gap has grown so wide that the map has stopped being a map - it's become a different document entirely, a statement of aspiration bearing roughly the relationship to reality that a travel brochure bears to the place it advertises.

I'm not a climate denier and I want to say so plainly, because the discourse is structured so that any questioning of climate policy gets sorted immediately into that category, and the sorting prevents the conversation I think we need to have.

The conversation isn't about whether climate change is real, because it is. It isn't about whether human activity contributes, because it does. It's about honesty - about the distance between what we say we're doing and what we're actually doing, and about what happens to a civilisation that institutionalises the distance.

Paris set a target of limiting warming to 1.5 degrees above pre-industrial levels. Reaching it required emissions cut roughly in half by 2030 and net zero by 2050. The target was announced with ceremony. Pledges were made, timelines set, press conferences held.

Emissions have risen every year since. The pledges aren't being met and aren't close to being met, and the gap between pledged reductions and actual emissions isn't narrowing - it's widening. The IEA, the IPCC and the UNFCCC all publish the numbers, the numbers are unambiguous, and the response to the numbers has been another round of pledges.

This isn't a failure of ambition. The pledges are enormously ambitious. It's a failure of execution so complete that failure isn't quite the right word, because failure implies an attempt that fell short. What I observe is the systematic substitution of commitment for action, announcement for implementation, map for territory.

Australia is a particularly vivid case. We're among the highest per-capita emitters on earth. We've announced some of the most ambitious reduction targets anywhere. Our actual emissions trajectory has been, at best, flat.

We export coal to the world while pledging reductions. We approve new gas projects while committing to net zero. We set renewable targets and then allow grid connection delays to strand new capacity for years at a time. The framework isn't internally coherent because it was never designed to be - it's designed to satisfy two constituencies at once, the environmental one demanding action and the economic one demanding continuation. Incoherence is the product of that design rather than a fault in it.

The result is a policy framework that is, in the precise sense of the word, fictional. It describes a reality that doesn't exist and isn't being built. It functions as a performance rather than a plan - a demonstration to domestic and international audiences that the government is Doing Something, where the something is the announcement.

I find this considerably more troubling than outright denial. A denier is at least making a claim that can be tested against evidence, which means the argument can be had and someone can lose it. The gap between map and territory is a different failure - a failure of integrity so normalised that it no longer registers as dishonest to anyone involved. Everyone knows the targets won't be met. The officials setting them know. The modellers assessing them know. The diplomats negotiating them know. And the knowledge produces no correction at all. It produces more sophisticated versions of the same performance.

The psychological function is obvious enough. Targets let people feel something is being done, and the feeling turns out to be sufficient. Whether anything is actually being done is a separate question the targets were never designed to answer.

There's one change I'd make, and it's deliberately small because grand redesigns are how this problem reproduces itself. Every emissions target should be published alongside the actual trajectory, on the same page, in the same document, updated annually, with the divergence stated as a number. Not a separate report from a separate agency that nobody reads. The same page.

The reason I'd start there is that the entire mechanism depends on the announcement and the outcome living in different documents, read by different people, at different times. Targets are launched at press conferences and assessed in technical appendices three years later. Put them side by side and the performance becomes much harder to sustain, because the gap acquires a number and numbers acquire arguments. It doesn't force anyone to act. It just makes not acting visible in the place where the credit is being claimed.

The pattern isn't confined to climate. Housing affordability targets announced and missed. Infrastructure timelines set and blown through. Health reform plans published and shelved. It's consistent enough that I'd call it a mode of governance - one whose primary output is narrative rather than policy, and whose success is measured by reception rather than outcome.

None of this is a communications problem or an ambition problem. It's an honesty problem, and until we're willing to say plainly what we're actually doing rather than what we're promising, the gap will keep widening in exactly the way it has for a decade.

The territory doesn't care about the map. It never has.
