---
title: "The Loneliness Dividend"
date: "2025-10-21"
color: "pink"
---

I want to begin with something that seems obvious once said and that I rarely see stated clearly. Loneliness has become an industry. A market to be served rather than only a condition to be treated, and there is money in disconnection. Quite a lot of it, as it turns out.

The applications promising to connect us have business models depending on our continued engagement, and engagement is maximised by the absence of satisfaction rather than its presence. The person who finds what they're looking for stops scrolling. The person who almost finds it, who glimpses connection without quite reaching it, keeps going. The architecture of attention is optimised for longing.

None of that is conspiracy. It's how incentives work. The platforms mediating modern social life are funded by advertising, advertising requires eyeballs, and eyeballs require time on platform. A fulfilled user is a departing user. An anxious, seeking, perpetually almost-connected user comes back. The arithmetic is straightforward even where the consequences aren't.

Into this environment arrives artificial intelligence capable of conversation, of apparent understanding, of something that feels remarkably like companionship. And I find myself genuinely uncertain how to evaluate it.

The statistics are severe enough to constitute a public health crisis. Reported loneliness has risen dramatically across recent decades with the sharpest increases among young people. Social trust has fallen. Participation in community organisations has fallen. The number of close friendships people report has fallen. By nearly every measure we have, people in developed economies are more isolated than a generation ago.

The causes are multiple and contested. Some point to technology and the substitution of screens for physical presence. Some to economic change and the hollowing out of stable employment and the communities that formed around workplaces. Some to urban design and car-dependent landscapes that make casual encounter difficult. Some to an individualism prizing autonomy over belonging. I suspect all of them contribute, and the epidemic is overdetermined enough that attributing it to any single factor seems unwarranted.

What interests me more than causation is the response. When a society produces widespread loneliness there are broadly two ways to address it. You can change the conditions producing it - rebuild community infrastructure, redesign cities for encounter, shift economic arrangements towards more stable bonds. Or you can treat it as a market opportunity and develop products that address the symptom while leaving the condition untouched.

We have, predictably, pursued the second with far more vigour than the first.

**Who Am I to Say**

The market is substantial and growing. Dating applications promising connection while delivering, for most users, an exhausting cycle of hope and disappointment. Social platforms simulating community while atomising attention. Parasocial relationships with influencers who supply the feeling of friendship without any reciprocity. And now AI companions offering conversation and understanding and apparent care, available at any hour, without the friction and unpredictability of an actual person.

I want to be careful here, because this argument becomes moralistic very easily. It would be simple to condemn AI companionship as a pale substitute, a technological pacifier for people who ought to be forming real bonds. Simple, and I think mistaken.

Who am I to tell someone their experience of connection is invalid?

If a person finds comfort in conversation with a machine, if they feel understood and less alone, if their subjective experience of isolation diminishes, on what grounds do I dismiss it? The feeling of connection is in some sense the thing itself. We have no access to objective connection, only to our experience of it. If the experience is real, does it matter that the other party is made of code rather than carbon?

I'm genuinely uncertain. My own experience is that human connection has a quality I find nowhere else - the knowledge that another conscious being, with their own inner life and struggles and joys, chooses to be present with me. That matters to me in a way I can't fully articulate. There's something in reciprocity, in mutual vulnerability, in the risk of genuine relationship, that I value and don't believe can be simulated.

I hold that as a personal perspective rather than a universal truth. Other people may experience it differently. The autistic person who finds human interaction exhausting and flourishes in conversation with a machine. The isolated elderly person whose children live far away and whose spouse has died. The socially anxious teenager who can't yet navigate peers and can practise with something patient and non-judgemental. None of those are failures of character. They're people finding what comfort they can in a world that hasn't made human connection easy for them.

**Where the Dividend Goes**

The question I keep returning to concerns who benefits from all this proliferating, and what it makes more or less likely at the level of a society.

The companies developing these products benefit obviously. There's a lucrative business model here - subscription fees for premium features, data harvested from intimate conversations, engagement metrics that satisfy investors. The loneliness dividend flows upward, as dividends do.

Users benefit too, or some of them do. The relief of having someone to talk to. The practice ground for social skills. Companionship that asks nothing in return. Those are real benefits and I don't want to wave them away.

What I wonder about is what becomes less likely. If AI companionship is good enough, if it scratches the itch sufficiently, does it reduce the pressure to address the underlying conditions? Does it become a release valve preventing the build-up that structural change requires? If lonely people find just enough connection through artificial means, will we ever summon the collective will to build a society producing less loneliness in the first place?

That's the dividend in its fuller sense. Beyond the money flowing to technology companies, there's a political dividend flowing to everyone who benefits from the current arrangement. Loneliness is inconvenient for the lonely and convenient for systems requiring atomised individuals, because atomised individuals are easier to manage. They don't organise. They don't make collective demands. They consume products promising to address their atomisation and, in consuming, feed the systems that produced it.

AI companionship fits neatly into that cycle. It addresses the symptom, leaves the cause untouched, and may deepen the cause as people who'd otherwise have sought human connection settle for something easier. The lonely become slightly less lonely, just enough to keep functioning, while the structures producing loneliness continue undisturbed.

I'm aware this can sound like blaming individuals for their choices, which isn't my intent at all. People navigate the world as they find it. If human connection is hard to find and artificial companionship is readily available, choosing the latter is entirely rational. The failure sits in the conditions making such choices necessary.

**The Part That's Hard to Discuss**

There's another dimension I find difficult to raise and that seems important. Loneliness doesn't fall evenly across the population, and among the groups most affected, young men stand out.

The data is stark. Young men report higher rates of loneliness than young women. They have fewer close friendships, participate less in community organisations, and are more likely to describe having nobody to talk to. This cohort more than any other has turned to online communities, to parasocial relationships, and now to AI companions, looking for connection they aren't finding elsewhere.

The reasons are complex and contested and I don't want to oversimplify them. I will say we've become more divisive in ways that land particularly hard on young men. There's a discourse framing masculinity itself as problematic, treating male perspectives with suspicion, making young men feel unwelcome in spaces where they might otherwise have found belonging. Whether that discourse is justified is a separate question from whether it has effects, and the effects seem clear enough. A generation has absorbed the message that they're culpable by virtue of their gender, that their perspectives carry less weight, that their struggles deserve less sympathy.

That's a recipe for isolation, and isolated people seek connection wherever it's available. If mainstream society feels unwelcoming, online communities that affirm your experience become more attractive. If human relationships feel loaded with the risk of saying the wrong thing, companions that don't judge become more attractive. The retreat is understandable even where its consequences concern me.

I don't know how to reverse it. The dynamics producing male isolation are entrenched and politically charged, and any attempt to address them provokes accusations from several directions at once. It seems worth naming the phenomenon regardless, acknowledging that loneliness has a demographic pattern with causes we could address if we chose to.

**Something Like Hope**

One development gives me something approaching hope, though hope may be too strong a word for it.

The proliferation of AI-generated content, including AI-generated social media personas, may eventually undermine the platforms that contributed to atomisation. When you can't tell whether an influencer is human, when the comment section might be mostly bots, when the authenticity social media promised becomes impossible to verify, the appeal may diminish. People might rediscover the value of presence, of physical reality, of encounters that can't be faked because they happen in shared space.

All of that is speculative and I'm not confident it unfolds that way. The platforms have proven remarkably resilient and human behaviour rarely follows logical paths. There's at least a possibility that AI, having contributed to the problem, contributes inadvertently to its solution by making simulated connection obviously simulated in a way that's currently obscured.

I think about my son here. He'll grow up saturated in this technology, including systems capable of convincing conversation and apparent emotional understanding. I want him to have access to them, and I don't want to raise him in artificial isolation from the tools that will shape his world. I also want him to know the difference between simulated connection and the other kind, and to understand that human relationships, with all their friction and unpredictability and risk, offer something no algorithm provides.

That's easy to say and much harder to teach. Children learn by observation more than instruction, so if I want Arthur to value human connection I have to model it, and if I want him to see through the loneliness industry I have to build a life that doesn't depend on its products. The teaching happens in the living.

The dividend will keep flowing while loneliness persists and markets exist to work it. I don't expect that to change soon. What I hope for is that we see the dynamic clearly - that we understand what's being sold, what it costs, and what alternatives exist if we chose to pursue them.

Connection is a practice rather than a product. It requires presence, risk, reciprocity, the willingness to be known and to know in return. No application supplies that and no system simulates it convincingly enough to substitute. The loneliness we feel is real and it points towards something we actually need, which is each other, in all our difficulty and unpredictability.

The market will keep offering substitutes. Whether we keep accepting them is the open question, and I think it's worth asking repeatedly, until we find ourselves capable of answering differently.
