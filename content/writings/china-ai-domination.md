I've been reluctant to write this because the conclusions feel almost too significant to commit to paper. The evidence has accumulated to a point where staying quiet starts to feel like its own form of dishonesty.

The question usually framing this discussion is whether China is winning the AI race, and I think it's poorly posed, because it assumes a race still being run. What I observe suggests a divergence that has already happened. One nation has reached something like escape velocity while others, including the United States and its allies, remain subject to constraints that may prove insurmountable.

Let me be precise about the term. In physics, escape velocity is the speed at which an object breaks free of gravitational pull - the point beyond which you're no longer fighting to stay aloft and are simply moving, with nothing left to pull you back. Applied to technological competition, it's the moment your advantages compound faster than anyone can close the gap. You've stopped racing. You're leaving.

I want to explain why I think that threshold may already be behind us.

Start with manufacturing. China accounts for roughly twenty-nine percent of global manufacturing output, which is more than an economic statistic. It's the physical substrate on which artificial intelligence has to be built. Every server rack, every cooling system, every specialised power management unit requires manufacturing capacity. You can't run algorithms on abstractions. You need hardware, and hardware needs factories.

The percentage understates the structural reality. What matters is that the supply chain depth has no parallel anywhere - the components, the materials, the tooling, the rare earth processing, the battery production. Layer upon layer of capability built over decades. When an American technology company designs a product it's assembled in China, and when that company attempts to move production elsewhere it discovers that elsewhere lacks the supplier ecosystem to make production viable. The dependency runs well below final assembly. It runs to the molecular level.

The pursuit of semiconductor independence is the clearest signal of intent. Western export controls were meant to constrain Chinese AI development by restricting access to advanced chips, and the response has been instructive. Acceleration rather than capitulation. When I look at designs like the CloudMatrix 384 I see a deliberate act of decoupling - an attempt to eliminate strategic vulnerabilities and establish a supply chain that can iterate without anyone else's permission. That's a parallel infrastructure owing nothing to anyone, and it goes well beyond catching up.

The CHIPS Act and allied efforts to reshore semiconductor manufacturing are real responses operating on different timescales. Building fabrication capacity takes years. Training the workforce takes longer. Establishing the supplier ecosystems takes longer still. The strategic intent is sound and the open question is whether execution can outpace the widening.

**The Move Nobody Priced Correctly**

Then there's open source, which I think is the most underestimated development in all of Western analysis.

Our technology industry has long assumed that proprietary secrecy drives progress. Keep the models closed, protect the weights, guard the training data. The logic is commercial: it creates moats, justifies valuations, and enables rent extraction from capabilities others can't replicate. OpenAI, Anthropic and Google have all pursued variations of it.

The Chinese approach inverts the whole thing.

Over eighteen months, DeepSeek has released models matching or approaching frontier capabilities and given them away. Qwen followed. Alibaba, Baidu and others contribute to an expanding ecosystem of open weights, open architectures, open research. The models fall somewhat short of the best closed Western systems, and they're close enough to be useful, and they're free.

Why give away what others charge for? The question exposes an assumption that may not hold. The value in AI may sit in the ecosystem forming around a model rather than in the weights themselves - the applications built on top, the talent that learns the architecture, the data flowing back through usage. By releasing high-performing models publicly, China has effectively conscripted the global developer community into a distributed research effort, where every fork and modification and community contribution accelerates the collective state of knowledge.

That's strategy rather than generosity. The fastest possible rate of improvement, achieved by externalising the research effort to the entire world. Meanwhile Western companies charge subscription fees for API access and watch developers migrate to free alternatives that are good enough for most purposes. The moat that was meant to protect commercial AI may turn out to be a wall keeping its builders isolated while the water rises outside it.

**Physics and Population**

Energy is where the physics becomes unforgiving. Large models require enormous power to train and to run - a single frontier training run can consume the annual electricity output of a small city, and inference at scale requires continuous power in quantities that strain existing infrastructure.

China's generation capacity exceeds its nearest competitor by roughly a factor of two and a half. That's a precondition rather than a detail. You can't build hyperscale data centres when your grid struggles with stability or when energy costs make the economics impossible. And the ability to deploy vast renewable projects alongside AI development means the advantage compounds, because China manufactures most of the world's solar panels and can deploy them domestically at costs nobody else can match. The limits on progress become theoretical rather than practical. Power is simply available.

Data follows a similar shape. A population of 1.4 billion, digitally connected, generating training data at a scale smaller nations can't replicate, with privacy constraints that don't bind the way ours do. Whether that's a feature or a defect depends entirely on your values, and for the narrow purpose of training systems it's an advantage.

The talent question is more nuanced than it first appears. For decades researchers flowed predominantly one way, from China to Western universities and companies, which created an assumption that we'd always have access to the best minds. That assumption is being tested. They're still coming, and many are also returning, because the opportunities in Chinese AI development are no longer obviously inferior - the funding, the compute access, the scale of deployment. The brain drain is becoming a circulation, and direction matters less when both ends of the pipe are productive.

What troubles me most is the integration between civilian innovation and military application. In the West we maintain something like a firewall, porous and imperfect and real. Defence procurement is slow and bureaucratic, technology transfer requires navigating regulatory complexity, and the Pentagon and Silicon Valley speak different languages while regarding each other with mutual suspicion. The Chinese approach eliminates the separation entirely. Civil-military fusion operates as an organising principle, breakthroughs flow immediately into defence applications, and an advance in computer vision becomes a military capability in months. The feedback loop tightens to a degree Western institutions aren't structured to match.

**Taking the Counterarguments Seriously**

I want to be careful about what I'm claiming. I'm not arguing this outcome is inevitable, or that nothing can be done, or that we should accept technological subordination. I'm arguing something more modest: the structural conditions favour one side in ways that aren't widely appreciated, and pretending otherwise does us no favours at all.

The counterarguments have merit and deserve stating properly. Western innovation culture remains vibrant. The best researchers still disproportionately choose American and European institutions. Chinese research quality, while improving fast, may lag in ways raw metrics don't capture. The institutional trust enabling scientific collaboration is stronger in open societies. The political constraints on Chinese technology companies create their own inefficiencies.

I'm simply not certain those are sufficient. The manufacturing base exists. The energy infrastructure exists. The strategic coordination exists. The open-source ecosystem expands daily. These are present realities rather than future possibilities.

For a country like Australia, caught between a security alliance with the United States and economic dependence on China, the implications are sharp. We lack the scale to compete independently, so our choice is whose infrastructure to depend on. The assumption that alliance relationships guarantee us access to frontier capability may prove optimistic if that capability is increasingly matched by systems available free to anyone with a download.

If there's one thing I'd have us do, it's stop measuring the race by benchmark scores and start measuring it by deployment. What fraction of the world's developers build on whose stack. What fraction of the world's new products embed whose models. That's the number that determines whose assumptions get encoded into the next decade of software, and it's the number nobody in Canberra is currently tracking.

History is full of moments when a civilisation's assumptions about its own permanence proved unfounded. The British Empire seemed unassailable until it wasn't. The Soviet Union appeared permanent until it collapsed. I don't know whether we're living through such a moment, and I think the question deserves considerably more serious attention than it gets.

The race may already be decided. What remains open is whether we're willing to look clearly at the evidence, or whether we prefer the comfort of assumptions that stopped holding some time ago.
