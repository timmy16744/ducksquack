title: "The Comfortable Dependency"
date: "2023-11-15"

---

I want to draw a dependency map - a diagram of what this country relies on, who supplies it, and what happens if the supply stops.

Security first, because everything else sits on top of it. Our strategic architecture is American: the ANZUS treaty, Pine Gap, the interoperability of our forces with American systems, the intelligence sharing of Five Eyes, and the nuclear submarines we're acquiring through AUKUS that we won't be able to build, maintain or operate without ongoing American support. Our defence rests on that alliance the way a building rests on its foundations, which is a very different arrangement from being independent and reinforced by one.

Trade is Chinese. Iron ore, coal, gas, agricultural products, education services, with the revenue funding a significant share of government expenditure. The relationship has been strained - the barley tariffs, the wine restrictions, the diplomatic freeze after we called for a COVID inquiry - and the iron ore has continued flowing because the mutual dependence runs deep enough to survive political friction. For now.

Technology is American, and increasingly American or Chinese with nothing in between. The platforms we use, the cloud infrastructure our businesses run on, the AI systems beginning to reshape the economy. We have no domestic platform of significance, no cloud infrastructure at scale, and no AI capability that registers anywhere on the global scoreboard. Our digital economy runs on infrastructure we don't own, can't influence and couldn't replace.

Manufacturing is everyone else. Electronics from China, vehicles from Japan and Korea and Thailand, pharmaceuticals from India and Europe, medical devices from the United States and Germany. The things filling our hospitals, powering our businesses and sustaining ordinary daily life all come from somewhere that isn't here.

Energy is the strangest entry on the list. We export it raw - coal, gas, uranium - and import it refined, embedded in the manufactured goods and processed materials we buy back. Our domestic market is fragile, with prices rising and reliability falling, while we sit on some of the most abundant energy resources on the planet.

Even food, where we're a net exporter and ought to be secure, has the same shape underneath. The farm produces the food. Fertiliser, agricultural chemicals, machinery and fuel - the inputs that let the farm produce anything at all - come from elsewhere.

**The Word for This**

Step back and look at what's on the page. Security: American. Trade: Chinese. Technology: American. Manufacturing: Chinese, Japanese, Korean, European. Energy: structurally dependent on foreign processing and infrastructure.

The word for that map isn't alliance, or partnership, or integration. It's dependency, and at the scale and comprehensiveness that characterises our position it's a form of vulnerability that no amount of diplomatic language disguises.

I've spent time in countries that take sovereignty seriously and the contrast is instructive. Singapore maintains a military formidable relative to its size, a domestic technology sector providing alternatives to foreign platforms, a strategic reserve system buffering against supply disruption, and a governance culture treating self-reliance as a first-order national priority. Israel builds its own weapons, develops its own technology, produces its own intelligence. The Nordic countries invest in domestic capability even where importing would be cheaper, because they've understood that cheapness bought through dependency isn't cheap.

We do none of this. We optimise for cost, buy the cheapest option from the most convenient supplier, and call it efficiency. We've outsourced security to America, trade to China, technology to Silicon Valley and manufacturing to everyone else, and we call the resulting arrangement an economy. It is an economy. It's also a web of dependencies comprehensive enough that sovereignty becomes, in practical terms, a polite fiction.

What does sovereign mean when you can't fuel your own economy for more than a few weeks without foreign supply? When your military can't operate without foreign systems, your businesses can't function without foreign cloud infrastructure, and your hospitals can't treat patients without foreign pharmaceuticals and devices? It means you have a flag, an anthem and a seat at the United Nations. It doesn't mean you control the systems your civilisation runs on.

**Comfort Is the Mechanism**

The comfort is the actual problem, and I think it deserves more attention than any individual dependency on the list.

We are comfortable. The dependencies haven't been tested by serious disruption. The supply chains function, the alliance holds, the trade continues. Everything works - the way a building with hidden structural faults works perfectly, right up until it doesn't. And because everything works, no minister is ever punished for failing to address any of this, and no minister who did address it would be rewarded, because the payoff lands two elections after they've left.

If I had to pick one intervention it would be an annual national dependency statement, tabled in Parliament, listing each critical input, its concentration by source country, and the estimated years to replacement. Published, debated, and unavoidable. A document fixes nothing on its own. It works because our actual problem is that no one is ever required to look at the whole map at once. Every dependency on that list was created by a sensible decision made in isolation by someone with no view of the total. A statement forces the total into a room where somebody has to answer for it.

The world that permitted this comfort - stable American hegemony, open trading systems, reliable supply chains, benign geopolitical competition - isn't the world now emerging. What's emerging is contested, fragmented, and increasingly organised around blocs that don't guarantee access to non-members.

If we enter that world carrying this map, we enter it as a client state, relying on the goodwill of others for the basic necessities of modern existence. That goodwill may well persist. It may not. The point is that we've built no fallback, and we've spent the decades in which one could have been built choosing comfort instead.

The comfortable dependency has served us for seventy years. Whether the next seventy resemble the last is the entire question, and I don't think the evidence is encouraging. The time to prepare for a different world is while the current one is still standing.
