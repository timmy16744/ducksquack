title: "The God-Shaped Hole"
date: "2021-12-20"

---

Two years of isolation. Two years in which the thin structures of communal life - the office, the pub, the gym, the casual encounter on the street - were suspended or restricted, leaving Australians, and people everywhere, largely alone with themselves.

What I notice, emerging from the worst of it, is that we were already alone before any of it started. The lockdowns didn't create the isolation. They removed the rituals that had been masking it. The commute. The water cooler. The Friday drinks. The small unremarkable interactions supplying just enough social contact to stop most people noticing how thin the fabric had worn.

The fabric was thin because something was taken out of it decades ago and nothing was installed in its place. I want to write about what was removed, and to do it from an unusual position: as someone who doesn't believe in the thing that went, and who thinks the removal has cost us more than we've admitted.

I grew up attending chapel at Prince Alfred College in Adelaide. Uniting Church. Hymns on Wednesday mornings. The Lord's Prayer before meals. The architecture of worship - stone and timber and stained glass - around me daily for years.

I didn't believe. Not as a child, not as a teenager, not now. I sat in the pews and found the hymns beautiful and the sermons sometimes interesting and the rituals strangely moving, and at no point did I believe there was a God listening or a Christ who had risen or a cosmic plan justifying the suffering I could already see around me.

I call myself agnostic and I mean it seriously, as an intellectual commitment rather than a polite way of saying atheist. I don't have sufficient information to determine whether there's a consciousness behind the architecture of the universe. The question strikes me as genuinely open - the strangeness of existence, the fine-tuning of physical constants, the hard problem of consciousness are not settled matters pointing clearly either way. Certainty in either direction seems to me a failure of humility.

What I don't believe is that any specific religion holds truths about the divine that the others lack. I find wisdom in Christianity: the emphasis on compassion, on forgiveness, on the dignity of every person. I find wisdom in Hinduism: the understanding that the self is less fixed than it appears, that reality has layers, that existence has depths materialist frameworks don't reach. I take the insights where I find them and don't worry much about the packaging.

This essay concerns what was lost when belief receded, and whether we've reckoned with the loss.

**Load-Bearing**

Religion, whatever else it was, provided infrastructure. Buildings mattered, and what mattered more was the infrastructure for human needs that don't disappear when the metaphysical framework goes.

Community. A weekly practice of sitting in a room with your neighbours and contemplating something beyond the immediate - something other than a networking event or a gym class or a meeting with an agenda. A gathering whose purpose was presence itself, being together in one space, oriented towards something larger than individual concerns. The secular world has produced no equivalent. We've tried community centres and clubs and co-working spaces and online forums, and none of them replicate the specific quality of regular, local, purpose-driven gathering.

Ritual. The marking of birth and death, of marriage and loss, of the transitions that structure a life. Religion took these seriously, surrounding them with ceremony and language honed over centuries and the accumulated weight of tradition. It said: this moment matters, pay attention, be present. The secular equivalents are thin by comparison. A registry wedding. A funeral where nobody knows quite what to say. The celebrations of life that aim for cheerful and land on awkward. We stripped the ritual from the transitions and left the transitions bare.

Moral conversation. I don't miss being told what to think about right and wrong, and I do miss the infrastructure for thinking about it. A shared vocabulary. A tradition of wrestling with ethical questions across centuries. The assumption that moral questions deserve sustained attention and that a community should dedicate regular time to how one ought to live. What replaced it is a culture war conducted on platforms optimised for outrage, where moral conversation has become moral performance and the goal is victory rather than understanding.

Confrontation with death. This is the one I feel most personally, because chronic illness forces a confrontation with mortality that the culture around me has no framework for.

Religion made you practise thinking about death every week. It said: you will die, this life is finite, what comes after is uncertain and this much is certain, it ends. And it provided a context for sitting with that knowledge, processing it in company rather than alone, developing some relationship with mortality that wasn't pure terror or pure denial.

Secular culture does none of this. It avoids death with a consistency that would be impressive if it weren't so damaging. We've outsourced dying to hospitals and aged care facilities, removed it from the home and the community, and built a culture terrified of the thing with no language for it, no practice for it, no communal space in which to process it.

When my headaches are at their worst, when the pain reaches the level where the body's own arithmetic starts weighing continuation against cessation, I find the culture around me has nothing to offer. No framework, no vocabulary, no ritual for sitting near death and finding footing there. I've had to assemble my own from scraps of philosophy and personal discipline and the sheer stubbornness of continuing.

**A Wall Removed**

I think the loneliness epidemic, the meaning crisis, the rise of conspiracy thinking and pseudo-religious movements are symptoms of a single condition rather than separate phenomena. A civilisation removed a load-bearing wall and installed nothing in its place.

Religion held up community, ritual, moral conversation and the confrontation with death. When it came out gradually over decades, through the entirely understandable process of Enlightenment rationality and scientific progress, the structures it supported didn't stay standing. They sagged. They cracked. Some collapsed.

The conspiracy movements proliferating now look to me like religions stripped of their redeeming features. They supply community - the in-group, the shared knowledge, the sense of belonging to something larger. They supply narrative - the hidden truth, the battle between good and evil, the apocalyptic timeline. They supply meaning: your life matters because you're one of the few who sees. They supply everything religion supplied except the parts that made it valuable, which were the emphasis on compassion, the discipline of humility, the confrontation with your own fallibility, and the moral seriousness the best traditions cultivated across centuries.

The AI companions beginning to appear fill a similar void - chatbots designed for emotional connection, virtual friends, digital therapists. They provide the feeling of being heard and known and mattering to someone, without the friction and unpredictability of an actual relationship. Secular confession without the confessor. Pastoral care without the pastor.

I'm not arguing for a return to religion, which would be pointless. You can't believe what you don't believe, and faith isn't a tap you turn on after noticing the plumbing is dry. The Enlightenment happened for good reasons and the scientific method is the most reliable tool for understanding reality humans have ever built. I wouldn't give it back.

I'm arguing for taking the loss seriously. For acknowledging that rationality alone doesn't furnish a full human life. For recognising that the needs religion served - community, ritual, moral seriousness, some relationship with death - are human needs rather than religious ones, and they persist whether or not the framework that once addressed them remains credible.

The work of building secular structures for those needs has barely started. We're a century into the secular experiment without having produced equivalents for what religion supplied at its best. Perhaps we can't. Perhaps these needs can only be met by frameworks including some element of the transcendent, some gesture towards the larger, some willingness to engage with mystery.

If so, the challenge is finding forms of transcendence that don't require intellectual dishonesty. Forms of community that are regular and local and oriented beyond the self. Rituals marking the passages of a life with the gravity they deserve. Moral conversation deeper than tribal signalling. A relationship with death that avoids both denial and despair.

The one I'd start with is death, because it's the most neglected and the most urgent. Every other need on that list has at least some secular experiment running somewhere. Almost nobody is building anything for this one, and every single person will need it.

I don't know what that looks like institutionally, and I know what it would have to do: make the contemplation of mortality a regular practice rather than an emergency, conducted with other people rather than alone, and available to someone who believes nothing at all. Death cafés gesture at it. Hospice culture understands it. Neither has reached the ordinary person who has, at this moment, no framework whatsoever and will need one on a day they can't predict.

The God-shaped hole is real, and pretending that deliveries and streaming and the scroll of the feed can fill it is its own form of denial. We took out a load-bearing wall. The building still stands, the cracks are spreading, people keep falling through the floor, and we keep being surprised when they do.

The building needs a new wall. I don't know what it looks like. I know it isn't the old one, and I know that pretending we don't need one has stopped working. The evidence sits in the loneliness statistics, in the mental health data, in the conspiracy movements, and in the quiet desperation of people who have everything the material world can supply and still feel that something essential is missing.

Something essential is missing. The honest response is to name it and begin, however awkwardly, to build.
