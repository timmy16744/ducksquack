title: "The Refinery That Closed"
date: "2022-08-15"
color: "orange"
---

The ExxonMobil refinery at Altona closed last year. The closure was a headline for about a day. What happened afterwards wasn't a headline at all, and I think it matters considerably more.

A refinery is a node in a network, which is the thing people miss when they picture a building. Feedstock comes in - crude oil, natural gas liquids, intermediary chemicals - and products go out: fuel, plastics precursors, chemical inputs for downstream manufacturers. Around it sits an ecosystem of companies that supply it, companies that depend on its output, companies that service its equipment, and workers who hold specialised knowledge about how that particular facility behaves. The refinery is the visible structure. The ecosystem is the thing that actually matters, and it's invisible until it's gone.

When Altona closed, the node was removed and the network didn't reroute. It degraded.

Downstream manufacturers that relied on Altona for feedstock had to find alternatives overseas. In a trade policy document that sounds trivial. In practice it means longer lead times, higher transport costs, different material specifications requiring retesting and recertification, and the loss of the close relationship that lets a supplier and a customer solve a production problem in an afternoon. A phone call to a plant twenty kilometres away becomes an email to a facility in Singapore, in a different time zone, with different priorities and different disruptions.

The workers dispersed, and this is the part I think gets least attention. Refinery operations require specialised knowledge - process engineering, safety systems, and the particular understanding of how one specific facility behaves under specific conditions. That knowledge isn't generic. A process engineer from Altona can't walk into another refinery and perform at the same level, because every facility has its own characteristics, its own history, its own accumulated workarounds. Some of those people found work in related industries, some retrained, some retired. None of them passed the specific knowledge of Altona to a successor, because there was no successor. Thirty years of accumulated understanding about one plant simply stopped existing on a Friday afternoon.

**Seven, Then Two**

Australia once had seven refineries. We now have two. Each decline has been treated as an isolated commercial decision - ExxonMobil deciding Altona was no longer viable, Shell deciding the same about Clyde, BP about Bulwer Island and Kwinana. Each closure was announced, reported, protested briefly, and absorbed into the general background of economic change.

The cumulative effect has been ignored rather than absorbed, and those are very different things to have done.

We now import most of our refined fuel from Singapore, South Korea and Japan. Our strategic fuel reserve - the fuel physically available in this country at any moment - is measured in weeks. The International Energy Agency recommends ninety days. We hold roughly twenty-five, and some of that is in transit on tankers currently somewhere on the ocean between Asian refineries and Australian ports.

If that supply were interrupted - by a conflict in the South China Sea, by a natural disaster damaging port infrastructure, by any event disrupting shipping through the straits our imports traverse - this country would begin running out of fuel in under a month. Not only motor fuel, though that would be the visible part. Aviation fuel. Diesel for trucks and trains and agricultural equipment. The fuel for the generators that back up hospitals when the grid fails. The fuel that moves food from farms to cities. We are an island economy that has arranged to hold three and a half weeks of the thing everything else depends on. When you say it plainly it sounds like a policy nobody would ever choose, and of course nobody ever did.

**Rational Decisions, Irrational Outcome**

Every one of those closures was defensible on its own terms. The companies were losing money and the economics didn't work. The alternatives - continued operation at a loss, or a government subsidy to maintain a capability the market wouldn't sustain - were unappealing to shareholders and treasurers alike, and I don't think the executives involved did anything wrong.

But the accumulation of individually rational commercial decisions has produced a nationally irrational strategic position. We optimised for commercial efficiency and achieved strategic vulnerability. The market did its job perfectly. The job it did left us unable to fuel our own economy for more than a few weeks without foreign supply.

The pattern repeats right across the industrial base. Each closure treated as a one-off, lamented briefly, forgotten. The tooling shipped offshore. The institutional knowledge dispersed. And no institution, no minister and no strategic framework anywhere is tracking the cumulative loss or calculating the point at which it becomes critical. Nobody is adding up.

That's the specific gap I'd close first, and it's smaller than it sounds. We need a standing national capability register: a maintained list of the industrial capabilities this country holds, which ones are down to a single provider, and what replacement would cost in years. Not a report - a register, updated continuously, published, and consulted before any closure passes without comment.

The reason I'd start there rather than with subsidies is that we currently have no mechanism for noticing. Each closure arrives as a commercial matter concerning one company, and there's no point in the system where anyone is required to ask what it means for the whole. A register makes the aggregate visible, and visible things acquire defenders. It's very hard to argue about whether a capability is worth keeping when nobody has written down that we have it.

A refinery is a capability rather than a facility, and capabilities don't return on request. Rebuilding one takes decades if it can be done at all - the supply chains re-established, the workforce retrained, the institutional knowledge redeveloped from nothing. The cost of rebuilding is always greater than the cost of maintaining, and the time required is always longer than the crisis allows. That asymmetry is the entire argument, and it's why this can't be left to the point where it becomes urgent.

We're dismantling our industrial capacity one reasonable decision at a time. Each decision is defensible. The trajectory they produce is not, and nobody has the job of saying so.
