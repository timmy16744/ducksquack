title: "The Credential and the Skill"
date: "2019-08-15"

A friend of mine graduated recently with an IT degree from a well-regarded Australian university. Three years of study, forty thousand dollars of HECS debt, a piece of paper with a crest on it. He applied for junior positions at a dozen companies. Most didn't respond, and the ones that did asked for two years of experience he didn't have and proficiency in tools his degree had never mentioned.

He's typical rather than unusual, which is the whole problem. The distance between what our universities teach and what the economy requires has grown wide enough that the degree has stopped functioning as a bridge between education and employment. It's become a toll booth - expensive to pass through, leading onto a road that may or may not connect to anywhere.

I want to be careful here, because criticising universities has become fashionable in certain circles and the fashionable version is usually shallow. Universities aren't worthless. The best of them provide something you can't get elsewhere: the discipline of rigorous thinking, exposure to ideas that dismantle your assumptions, the experience of sustained intellectual effort across years. These things are valuable, and I'd argue they're more valuable now than ever, as the economy grows more complex and the problems harder.

The difficulty is that those values are incidental to the system rather than central to it. The system is optimised for throughput. For certification. For producing graduates at a volume that satisfies government funding models and international student revenue targets. The degree has become the product, and the product has come loose from the thing it's supposed to represent.

I work in product design, and from that vantage point the Australian university system looks like a product with a fatal flaw: the credential is the value proposition, and the credential is losing value.

**Where Competence Was Actually Built**

The divergence between credential and competence isn't new, and it's widening. I meet graduates regularly who can recite frameworks and cite textbooks and cannot solve a problem they haven't previously encountered. They've been trained to pass assessments, which is a genuine skill, and it isn't the skill the economy pays for. The economy pays for the ability to do useful things - to build, to fix, to create value someone will hand over money for. That requires practice, failure, iteration, and the kind of tacit knowledge you can only acquire by doing the thing badly at first and gradually less badly over a sustained period.

The traditional mechanism for acquiring it was the entry-level job. You graduated with a credential signalling basic competence, then spent years somewhere the real learning happened. The credential was a ticket to the training ground, never the training itself. The university taught you the vocabulary and the workplace taught you the language.

That mechanism is breaking down, and it's breaking down for reasons that will accelerate rather than reverse.

In my own field, the tasks that once occupied junior designers - wireframing standard interfaces, writing basic specifications, running routine user research - are increasingly done by tools that didn't exist five years ago. The junior position existed because the work needed a human. When it stops needing a human, the position goes, and the training ground goes with it.

The same pattern is visible everywhere I look. Junior lawyers who'd have spent years on document review and legal research, displaced by software that does it faster and cheaper. Junior accountants whose role was data entry and basic analysis, made redundant by automated systems. The entry-level work that justified hiring a credential-holder and turned them into a competent professional is evaporating.

This leaves the university system facing a situation it wasn't designed for. If the credential no longer opens the door to the training ground, and the training ground is where competence actually gets built, then the credential leads nowhere. The university has sold you a map to territory that no longer exists.

**The Incentive Problem**

What replaces it? I genuinely don't know, and I'm suspicious of the confident answers on offer.

The autodidact's path has always existed in theory - teach yourself, build a portfolio, demonstrate competence through work rather than certification. Some people thrive on it. They're typically self-directed, resourceful, comfortable with ambiguity, and driven by something internal that doesn't need external structure. In my experience they're also a minority.

Most people learn best inside structured environments with clear expectations and external accountability. They need a curriculum, a progression, feedback from someone more experienced, and the social pressure of peers working alongside them. The university supplied part of that and the workplace supplied the rest. When both weaken - the university by optimising for credentials, the workplace by eliminating the positions that trained people - the majority are left with no viable path from qualification to capability.

Our universities carry an additional complication that I think is underappreciated. They've become, in practical terms, an export industry, with international students as a major revenue source. The incentive structure rewards enrolment volume because volume drives revenue, and quality, where it's measured at all, gets assessed through metrics that capture credential production rather than competence: graduation rates, satisfaction surveys, employment statistics counting any employment regardless of relevance.

It's an optimisation function, much like the social media algorithms I've written about elsewhere. The system is doing precisely what it's built to do. It produces credentials at scale, and the growing disconnection between those credentials and the skills the economy needs is an externality the system has no mechanism for noticing.

The change I'd argue for is narrow: fund universities partly on what their graduates can demonstrably do three years out, assessed by something other than the institution awarding the degree. Not employment rates, which count any job at all and are gamed accordingly. Actual capability, sampled and independently verified.

I'd start there because every other reform proposal asks universities to act against their own funding model out of principle, and institutions never do that for long. Change what the money responds to and the curriculum follows within a decade. Leave the money where it is and you'll get sincere reviews, revised strategies, and the same graduates.

This is about to get considerably worse. Artificial intelligence is advancing at a rate that will transform knowledge work inside the next decade and possibly inside a few years, and when it does, the skills our universities currently teach - analysis, research, synthesis, the cognitive work forming the backbone of professional education - are the ones most directly in the path. The technology may never do them better. It will do them cheaply enough that the economic value of a human doing them collapses, which is sufficient.

At that point the credential won't merely be disconnected from competence. It'll be disconnected from economic value, which is a different order of problem. A degree in a field whose core tasks can be performed by software has stopped being a qualification and become a receipt for a purchase that already depreciated.

We've confused the menu with the meal. The credential is the menu and the skill is the meal, and the economy is about to start checking what's actually on the plate. A great many people are going to find that the menu was beautifully printed.
