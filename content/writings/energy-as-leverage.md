Russia invaded Ukraine three weeks ago. When we look back on this period in the decades to come, I think we'll realise it was the moment Europe finally discovered what its cheap energy had actually cost - and that the discovery arrived about twenty years too late to be useful.

I've paid close attention to energy markets for most of my working life, because I've always had the conviction that the physical basis of an economy tells you more about its future than its financial statements ever will. Money is a claim on things. Energy is the thing itself. A country can print the first and cannot print the second, and that asymmetry decides more of history than we like to admit.

Germany built its industrial economy on cheap Russian gas, and for decades this was presented as sophisticated policy. Diversification of supply. Engagement through trade. The theory that economic interdependence moderates behaviour - that a country selling gas to Europe wouldn't attack a European neighbour, because the consequences would be too severe to contemplate.

The theory has now been tested. Russia has invaded a European country while continuing to sell gas to the continent nominally opposing the invasion, and the gas is still moving through Ukrainian pipelines while the war is fought above them. Europe funds the war machine through energy purchases on Monday and sanctions it through financial restrictions on Tuesday. When you stop and look at it directly, we've built the most sophisticated economies in human history on a fuel we have to ask permission to receive. It's remarkable it held together as long as it did.

**The Shape of the Error**

The lesson here has very little to do with Russia. What's on display is a category of error that recurs wherever a country builds critical dependence on a strategic competitor and assumes the competition will never be exercised because the money is too good on both sides. That assumption has underwritten Western strategic thinking since the end of the Cold War. It sits beneath the European gas relationship, beneath the Western economic relationship with China, and beneath Australia's entire strategic posture.

We export iron ore, coal, gas, agricultural products and education services to China, which is our largest trading partner by a substantial margin. We import electronics, pharmaceuticals, medical devices and industrial components from the same country. We sell raw materials to a strategic competitor and buy finished goods back from them, and we've told ourselves the arrangement is too valuable for either side to disrupt. That is precisely the sentence Berlin was saying in 2019.

Europe is now learning what happens when the assumption fails, and the education is expensive. Gas prices have spiked. Energy costs are cascading through every sector. The scramble for alternatives - LNG terminals, coal restarts, nuclear reconsiderations - reveals the real constraint, which is time. Energy infrastructure takes years to build. The dependence was assembled over decades and can't be unwound in weeks, however urgent the need becomes.

Nobody knows for certain how the next decade of this plays out, and the honest experts disagree with each other. China may need our iron ore for another twenty years, or may not. The Indo-Pacific may stay commercially governed, or may not. When uncertainty is this wide and the downside is this severe, the sensible strategy is to build the capacity to be wrong without being ruined, which is a different discipline from confidence in either direction.

**What Australia Should Actually Do**

The specific step I'd like to see is narrow, cheap and almost boring, which is usually the mark of a good one. Every major energy and critical-input procurement decision in this country should be required to publish a concentration figure alongside its price: what share of this supply comes from a single country, and what would replacement cost in dollars and in months.

That's it. No tariffs, no bans, no decoupling. Just a number, published, next to the price, in every business case above a threshold.

The reason I think this matters more than it sounds is that dependence is currently invisible at the moment of decision. A procurement officer choosing the cheapest supplier is doing their job correctly and is not asked to price the option they're quietly handing to that supplier's government. Nobody in Germany ever decided to make the country's foreign policy contingent on Gazprom. Thousands of people made individually sensible purchasing decisions over thirty years, and the contingency was the sum of them. You cannot manage a risk that never appears on any page.

Once the number exists, the rest follows without anyone needing to be brave. Departments can be given concentration ceilings the way they're given budget ceilings. Insurers can price it. Boards can be asked about it. A decade of that would move the country further than any single grand strategy, because it works with the grain of how these decisions actually get made rather than against it.

Alongside it we need to shorten the time it takes to build. Europe's real problem this winter is timing rather than scarcity: every alternative takes five years and the crisis took five days. A country that can build an import terminal or a generator in two years has strategic options that a country needing eight simply does not have, regardless of what either country decides.

**The Test Hasn't Arrived Yet**

Australia hasn't had its moment of discovery. The iron ore still flows, the assumptions remain untested, and the 2020 trade disputes - the barley tariffs, the wine restrictions - were a demonstration too small to change anyone's mind.

That's the opportunity, and it's why this is worth writing now rather than afterwards. Europe is paying the tuition for a lesson we can have for nothing. Every dependence this country has was built deliberately, over years, by people making locally sensible decisions, which means every one of them can be unwound the same way by people making better ones. None of it is fixed. It's simply unexamined.

The gas still flows through Ukraine. The iron ore still flows north. What we do with the years before our own test arrives is entirely ours to choose, and I'd much rather we chose it than discovered it.
