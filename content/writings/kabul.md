The photographs from Kabul this week don't need commentary. They supply their own. People clinging to the fuselage of a C-17 as it taxied. The crowds pressed against the airport perimeter. Helicopters lifting off an embassy roof in images that rhyme so precisely with Saigon in 1975 that the rhyming feels almost deliberate, as though history occasionally exercises a sense of irony.

Twenty years. Two trillion dollars. The longest war in American history, ending in a withdrawal indistinguishable from a rout.

I want to be careful about what I think these images mean, because the commentary is already sorting itself into partisan categories - Biden's failure, Trump's deal, the Pentagon's miscalculation - and the sorting will bury the structural observation underneath it.

The structural observation is that the United States committed to a project it couldn't complete, sustained the commitment long past the point of rational justification, and then withdrew under conditions that showed the commitment had achieved far less than anyone was willing to say while it was ongoing. The Afghan army, trained and equipped at enormous cost, dissolved in days. The government, propped up for two decades, collapsed before the last American aircraft cleared Kabul airspace. The whole edifice - the democracy-building, the institution-building, the training, the infrastructure - evaporated as though it had never been there at all.

This is imperial overextension made visible. Not in a think-tank paper or a declassified cable. On television, in real time, and unmistakable.

Rome maintained legions across three continents, and the commitment was sustainable while the empire was expanding and the spoils funded further expansion. It stopped being sustainable when the expansion ended and the commitments didn't. The legions stayed deployed, the costs accumulated, the treasury strained, and eventually the withdrawals began. They came as collapses rather than orderly retrenchments, one frontier at a time, each revealing that the control had always been thinner than the maps suggested.

**What This Means From Here**

What matters most from where I sit in Adelaide isn't the fate of Afghanistan. That's a tragedy, and it's Afghanistan's tragedy, and I don't presume to understand its internal dynamics from the other side of the world. What matters is what the withdrawal tells us about the nature of American commitment, because American commitment is the foundation our security rests on.

Every nation sheltering under that umbrella watched Kabul fall and ran the same calculation. If America will commit to Afghanistan for twenty years, spend two trillion dollars, lose thousands of lives, build bases and train armies and install governments - and then leave in a manner suggesting the whole project was a polite fiction maintained for domestic reasons - what is any American commitment actually worth?

That's a strategic question rather than a rhetorical one, and it carries concrete implications for every country relying on American power projection.

ANZUS assumes the United States will defend Australia if Australia is threatened. That assumption has been the cornerstone of our defence policy for seventy years. It has let us maintain a military small relative to our geography and our potential threats. It has let us avoid the hard choices about independent capability that Israel, Singapore and the Nordics have all made as a matter of survival.

Kabul doesn't invalidate the alliance, and I want to be precise about that, because the argument I'm making isn't that we should abandon it. What Kabul provides is evidence about the character of American commitment that ought to be incorporated into our calculations. The evidence suggests those commitments are conditional, that the conditions can change, and that when they change the withdrawal can be faster and more complete than allies expect.

Australian soldiers served in Afghanistan. They fought and some of them died in a war their ally has now abandoned in a way that makes the fighting and dying look pointless. I don't say that to dishonour the service, which was real and costly and performed with the professionalism our military has consistently shown. I say it because the distance between the service rendered and the outcome achieved ought to provoke a reckoning about the framework within which the service was offered.

We sent our people because the alliance asked. We'll be asked again. The only question is whether we assess the next request carrying the same assumptions we carried into Afghanistan, or whether we update them in light of what we've just watched.

**The Data Point Nobody Mentions**

The American public's response to Kabul may be the most significant piece of evidence in the whole episode, and it's had almost no attention here. The response was, overwhelmingly, indifference. Polls showed consistent support for withdrawal regardless of the manner of it. Two decades of war concluded in humiliation, and the public shrugged.

An empire whose citizens don't care about losing a twenty-year war is an empire whose commitments elsewhere should be evaluated with very clear eyes. That isn't a criticism of Americans, who were told for twenty years that the war was going well and eventually stopped believing it. It's an observation about what sustains a security guarantee, which in a democracy is ultimately public willingness to bear a cost. That willingness is a variable, and we have been treating it as a constant for seventy years.

What I'd argue for isn't a rupture. The alliance has served this country well and may serve it well for decades more. What I'd argue for is a quality of attention I don't currently see anywhere in Australian strategic discourse - a serious, funded, public assessment of what we could actually do alone, for how long, and at what cost. Not because we expect to be alone. Because a country that has never asked the question has no idea what its own answer would be, and that ignorance is itself a strategic position, chosen by default.

The photographs from Kabul deserve more than a week of coverage and a return to business as usual. They deserve to be studied and absorbed and worked into how we think about our own security, and the window for doing that calmly is open right now, while nothing is being demanded of us.

The umbrella may not have folded. But anyone who looked at Kabul and didn't hear fabric tearing wasn't paying close enough attention.
