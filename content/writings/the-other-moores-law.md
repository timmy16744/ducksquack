Something is happening in Chinese artificial intelligence that most Western observers haven't fully registered, and I want to describe it as clearly as I can - without the alarmism that usually attaches to this subject, and without the complacency that usually follows the alarmism.

In August, Baidu released Ernie Bot, a large language model performing on most benchmarks within striking distance of GPT-4. What makes that notable isn't Ernie Bot itself. It's what it represents: the closing of a gap that looked, eighteen months ago, like it was widening.

When ChatGPT launched at the end of 2022, the assumption across Western technology circles was that American AI was so far ahead that serious Chinese competition was years away. The reasoning was superficial and genuinely compelling. The United States had the best researchers, the most advanced chip design, the deepest capital markets, and the open, competitive, intellectually free environment that innovation is supposed to require. I believed a version of it myself.

The reasoning held up in parts and failed in exactly the place that mattered, and it's worth tracing the structure carefully, because structure determines trajectory and trajectory is what decides a race.

Start with manufacturing. China physically manufactures most of the world's electronics. Design talent is distributed globally and always has been; the fabrication is what has concentrated. The fabs, the assembly lines, the supply chains that turn a design into a product exist overwhelmingly in China and Taiwan. That base provides something no amount of research talent substitutes for, which is the ability to build at scale, quickly, and to iterate physically rather than on paper.

Then energy. China has invested more in energy infrastructure over the last decade than any nation in history. Solar, wind, nuclear, coal - the mix isn't clean and the scale is extraordinary. Training requires enormous compute and compute requires enormous power, and China is building the capacity to run the next generation of this technology while Western nations, tangled in planning processes and community opposition, are not.

Then coordination. The Chinese system removes the separation between civilian innovation and strategic application that characterises Western approaches. Technology transfer from Silicon Valley to the Pentagon is slow, bureaucratic and frequently adversarial. In China, civil-military fusion is an organising principle rather than a slogan, and a commercial breakthrough becomes a military capability in months rather than years.

Then data, where China operates at a scale and permissiveness that Western democracies can't match. More data used with fewer restrictions produces better models. I want to be precise that this is a technical observation and not a moral one - I wouldn't trade the Western position on privacy to close the gap, and I don't think we should.

And then open source, which is the development I find most strategically significant and the one that gets the least attention. Rather than competing with American closed models on their own terms, Chinese labs have adopted open release as a posture. If you can't control the frontier, democratise it. Make the baseline free and compete on integration, deployment and scale.

That last move is the one that actually wins, and I think Western commentary has the whole scoreboard wrong because of it. A closed model two points ahead on a benchmark reaches the customers who can pay for it. An open model slightly behind reaches everyone - gets built into ten thousand products, trains a generation of engineers on its conventions, and quietly becomes the default assumption of an entire ecosystem. Leadership was never the frontier. Leadership is the baseline, and we've been measuring the wrong number for two years.

**Taking the Counterarguments Seriously**

The case against everything I've just written has real merit and I don't want to wave it through.

Western innovation culture remains genuinely vibrant. The best researchers still disproportionately choose American institutions. Chinese research quality, while improving fast, may still lag in ways benchmarks don't capture - in the creative, paradigm-shifting work that produces breakthroughs rather than increments. The institutional trust that makes scientific collaboration possible is stronger in open societies, and that compounds over decades in ways that are easy to underrate.

These points are real. I'm just not certain they're sufficient. The manufacturing base exists. The energy infrastructure exists. The coordination exists. The open-source ecosystem is expanding every month. Those are present realities rather than future possibilities, and betting against them means betting that research culture beats industrial capacity - which is a bet history doesn't obviously support.

Nobody knows how this resolves. The people I most respect on both sides of it disagree, and the honest position is that the outcome depends on decisions that haven't been made yet.

**Australia's Position**

For us the implications are sharp. We're caught between a security alliance with the United States and an economic dependence on China. We have no independent AI capability of any significance, no chip fabrication, and no manufacturing base that could support the physical infrastructure this technology runs on. The only choice in front of us is whose AI infrastructure we depend on, because whether to depend on someone's was settled years ago without anyone voting on it.

The comfortable assumption in Canberra is that the alliance guarantees access - that American technology will be shared with Australian allies as a matter of course. I think that's optimistic in a way that could cost us badly. Technology sharing follows strategic interests and strategic interests shift. If Chinese capabilities match or exceed Western ones, the calculus changes in ways that are hard to predict and unlikely to favour small partners. An alliance is a relationship, not a subscription.

What I'd argue for is narrow and achievable. We should build sovereign compute: enough national capacity to train, fine-tune and run serious models on our own soil. A frontier lab is beyond us and we couldn't staff one. It's expensive in the way a mid-sized road project is expensive, which is to say entirely affordable by a country that has decided something matters. Compute follows power, and we have more sun, wind, uranium and gas than almost anywhere on earth. Right now we're exporting the input to an industry we could be hosting.

Alongside that we should take the open-source ecosystem seriously as strategy rather than as a curiosity. We'll never lead the frontier and we could be excellent at adapting open models to specific domains - medicine, agriculture, mining, defence - which is where most of the economic value actually lands anyway. And we should stop losing our best people to San Francisco and London for reasons that are entirely rational and entirely fixable.

History is full of moments when a civilisation's confidence in its own technological superiority turned out to be unfounded, and the recurring feature of those moments is that the confidence felt like an observation right up until it didn't.

I don't know whether this is one of them. What I'm confident about is that Australia has chosen, deliberately or by default, to be a spectator in the most consequential technology race in human history - and that spectating is a decision we renew every year we don't act on it. We are not too late. We simply aren't trying yet, which is a much easier problem to fix and a much harder one to admit.
