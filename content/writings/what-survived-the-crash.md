---
title: "What Survived the Crash"
date: "2023-06-15"
color: "orange"
---

FTX collapsed in November. Sam Bankman-Fried, who'd been on magazine covers and testifying before Congress and donating millions to political campaigns, turned out to be running something between a Ponzi scheme and a casino funded with customer deposits. Eight billion dollars of customer money, missing. The second-largest cryptocurrency exchange on earth, gone inside a week.

Before FTX there was Terra/Luna, a so-called stablecoin that proved to be neither stable nor coined, whose algorithmic peg to the dollar unwound in a death spiral that destroyed forty billion dollars of notional value in seventy-two hours. Before that, Three Arrows Capital had leveraged itself into oblivion. Before that, a long parade of smaller collapses, rug pulls and straightforward frauds that had been the background noise of the industry for years and that the bull market made easy to ignore.

The hype has collapsed. The fraud has been exposed. The influencers who promoted projects they didn't understand to audiences who trusted them have moved on to promoting other things. The industry as it existed in 2021 - the Bored Apes, the yield farming, the protocols promising implausible returns, the whole superstructure of speculation and manufactured urgency - is largely dead, and almost nobody outside it is mourning.

Good. Most of it deserved to die.

And yet Bitcoin is still here. The network runs. The blockchain hasn't been hacked, not in fifteen years, not once. The protocol keeps producing blocks every ten minutes, executing transactions and maintaining consensus across a distributed network that no single entity controls. The price has fallen from 69,000 dollars to roughly 25,000 as I write this, and the system is functioning exactly as it always has. The crash happened in the price. It didn't happen in the infrastructure.

I think that distinction gets lost in the entirely justified satisfaction of watching fraudsters exposed, and it's worth separating carefully, because the two things have almost nothing to do with each other.

**What the Fraud Concealed**

The fraud was real and the speculation was absurd. The industry attracted a concentration of charlatans, grifters and incompetents impressive even by the standards of financial manias. A regulatory vacuum was exploited by people whose primary skill was the appearance of legitimacy, and the harm they caused - to retail investors who lost savings, to institutions that lost credibility, to the whole idea of digital finance - was substantial and in many cases unforgivable.

But the demand signal underneath it is genuine, and I think it's a serious mistake to read the collapse of the fraud as the resolution of the question. Millions of people across dozens of countries decided they wanted monetary infrastructure that no government could debase or seize. That desire didn't originate with Bitcoin and it isn't going to disappear with the crypto winter, because it's a response to something real: the well-documented observation that governments debase currencies, that central banks serve institutional interests not always aligned with ordinary people, and that the monetary system as constituted transfers wealth from savers to borrowers and from the young to the old in ways nobody ever voted on.

I wrote two years ago about the money printer and the emperor - the parallel between quantitative easing and Roman currency debasement, and the mechanism by which newly created money flows into asset prices, enriching owners and impoverishing workers. It's a hidden tax with no ballot attached, and it's the specific thing Bitcoin was built in response to.

**Being Honest About the Limits**

None of that makes Bitcoin the answer, and I want to be careful here, because the strongest version of the sceptical case deserves stating.

The energy consumption is a real problem rather than a talking point. The volatility makes it close to useless as a medium of exchange, which is one of the three things money is supposed to do. The concentration of holdings among early adopters recreates precisely the kind of unearned advantage the technology was meant to escape. And the limitations may turn out to be fundamental rather than solvable - the honest position is that nobody knows yet, and fifteen years is not long enough to tell.

What I'd hold onto is narrower. The search for alternatives to institutional money isn't going away, because the conditions driving it are intensifying rather than resolving. Debasement continues. Trust in institutions keeps declining. The distributional unfairness of the current system compounds every year it runs.

From Australia the monetary picture has a particular shape worth naming. Our national wealth is denominated almost entirely in a housing market priced in Australian dollars, managed by a Reserve Bank that follows the Federal Reserve's lead, subject to the same inflationary pressures and the same debasement dynamics as every other fiat currency. Our monetary sovereignty is about as thin as our manufacturing sovereignty. We don't set the price of money. We follow the people who do, and we've arranged our entire national balance sheet around the assumption that they'll keep making decisions we can live with.

The hype collapsed and the fraud was exposed, and the underlying demand for monetary alternatives didn't go anywhere. It went quiet, which isn't the same as going away.

So the question was never whether people will keep looking for alternatives to institutional money. They will, for reasons that have very little to do with technology and everything to do with the arithmetic of the system they're living inside.

The question is what they find, and whether the people building the next attempt have learned anything from watching this one burn.
