---
title: "The Narrow Strait"
date: "2019-05-10"
color: "blue"
---

I've been looking at maps lately. The political ones show borders and capitals and the colourful fiction of national sovereignty. I've been looking at the other kind - the ones that show what actually moves.

Trade routes. Shipping lanes. The physical corridors the global economy flows through. When you look at those maps you notice something the political ones conceal completely, which is that the entire system passes through a handful of very narrow places.

The Strait of Hormuz is thirty-three kilometres wide at its narrowest, and roughly twenty percent of the world's oil moves through it. Iran sits on one side, Oman and the UAE on the other, and tankers make the passage every day while the global economy quietly depends on their doing so.

The Strait of Malacca narrows to less than three kilometres of navigable channel, and about a quarter of all global trade by volume passes through it. Virtually everything moving between the Indian Ocean and the Pacific - oil from the Gulf to East Asia, manufactured goods from China to Europe - funnels through that corridor between Malaysia and Indonesia.

The Suez Canal is artificial but a chokepoint all the same, carrying roughly twelve percent of global trade. The Taiwan Strait is narrower in strategic significance than in physical geography, and Taiwan produces the overwhelming majority of the world's advanced semiconductors - the chips in your phone, your car, your medical equipment, your infrastructure, all fabricated on an island in contested waters. The Bosphorus. The Panama Canal. The Danish Straits. The list runs on, and the pattern is unmistakable: globalisation, the system supplying nearly everything we consume and depend on, passes through geographical needles, and the needles are narrow.

I find this fascinating and alarming in roughly equal measure, and I think the two reactions are connected.

Fascinating because it exposes the physical reality underneath the economic abstraction. We discuss globalisation as though it were a system of information - a network of financial flows existing in spreadsheets and databases. It isn't. It's ships on water and fuel in tanks and steel containers stacked on decks, navigating passages that have mattered strategically since people first put cargo on boats. The geography hasn't changed since the Phoenicians. Only the stakes have, and they've changed by several orders of magnitude.

Alarming because nobody prices the fragility. The global economy operates on the assumption that these passages stay open. Shipping costs are calculated on uninterrupted transit. Supply chains are designed for efficiency rather than resilience. Just-in-time manufacturing, adopted by nearly every industry on earth, assumes components arrive when needed, which assumes the ships carrying them cross the straits without delay.

What happens when they don't? The honest answer is that nobody is entirely sure, because it hasn't happened at meaningful scale in the modern era. There have been disruptions - Suez closed from 1967 to 1975, rerouting trade around Africa and reshaping shipping economics, and piracy in Malacca has periodically pushed up insurance and required naval patrols. But a genuine closure of a major chokepoint, whether through conflict in the Taiwan Strait, a confrontation in Hormuz or a blockade of Malacca, would be unprecedented in the age of integrated supply chains. We have no data because we have never run the experiment, which is exactly the situation in which confidence is least warranted.

For Australia none of this is abstract. We're an island continent, everything we don't produce arrives by sea, and the lanes it arrives through run across the most contested corridors on earth. Our imports from Northeast Asia transit the South China Sea and the approaches to the Taiwan Strait. Our energy imports come through passages subject to the strategic interests of nations whose priorities may not match ours. Our exports follow the same routes in reverse.

We've built an economy that depends utterly on maritime trade moving uninterrupted through straits we don't control, can't defend independently and have no plan to do without.

That dependence isn't secret. Strategists and defence planners know it, and the vulnerability appears in white papers and strategic assessments with the regularity of an annual report. Awareness simply hasn't produced action. We haven't built domestic manufacturing that could substitute for interrupted imports. We haven't diversified our routes to reduce exposure to any single corridor. We haven't invested in the strategic reserves - fuel, medical supplies, critical components - that would buffer against a serious disruption.

The step I'd take first is the least ambitious one available, which is why I think it might actually happen. Build the fuel reserve. Ninety days, held onshore, as the International Energy Agency recommends and as we currently don't come close to. We hold weeks. Not months, weeks, and some of that is on tankers still somewhere on the ocean.

I'd start there rather than with a grand industrial strategy for two reasons. It requires no new capability, no workforce we don't have, and no decade of construction - only tanks and a decision. And it converts an abstract argument about chokepoints into a physical asset that exists whether or not the next government agrees with the reasoning. Strategic arguments get relitigated every three years. Fuel in a tank doesn't.

Every previous era of globalisation ended. The Mediterranean networks of the ancient world. The Silk Road. The British Empire's trading system. Each was disrupted or destroyed by conflict, by shifting power, by the closure of the routes that sustained it. There's no law of nature making our version permanent, and the people living inside each of those systems were as confident of its permanence as we are of ours.

I'm not predicting disruption - prediction at this level of complexity is a fool's game and I'd rather not play it. What I observe is a system assuming continuity in conditions where continuity isn't guaranteed, and a country that has made itself extraordinarily dependent on that assumption while building nothing at all for the possibility that it's wrong.

The straits are narrow, the ships are large, and the consequences of obstruction are severe. We proceed as though the passages will stay open because they always have, which is a form of reasoning that works perfectly until the day it doesn't. We have time to build the buffer. What we don't have is any reason to think the time is unlimited.
