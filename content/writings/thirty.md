I turned thirty this week. People turn thirty every day. I've been sitting with it, and what I find when I sit with it is impatience rather than nostalgia or regret - a sense that the shape of the first three decades was preparation for something, and that the something is arriving faster than the preparation anticipated.

I want to trace the shape honestly. The actual version rather than the curated one.

I grew up in Australia, which means I grew up inside an assumption. That things would continue. That the structures around you were permanent. That the world you inherited was, give or take, the world you'd pass on. It was embedded in everything - the suburban architecture, the school curriculum, the career advice, the national self-image. We were the lucky country, and luck was the plan.

The cluster headaches started when I was young enough that the medical system treated me as a curiosity rather than a patient. The literature calls them suicide headaches and that's clinical description rather than hyperbole. I won't dwell on the pain here, having written about it elsewhere and expecting to write about it again. What matters for this essay is what the pain taught me, which is that systems fail.

Not occasionally. Not at the margins. Structurally. The Australian healthcare system failed me repeatedly across years - wrong diagnoses, wrong medications, procedures that should never have been performed, specialists who saw narrow slices of a person and never spoke to each other. A system built for acute problems meeting a chronic one and defaulting again and again to interventions that made things worse, because intervening was what it knew how to do.

I learned to build my own infrastructure. Exercise regimes, dietary controls, environmental management, the careful calibration of every input to a body that punishes miscalculation savagely. That was survival engineering rather than wellness culture, and it gave me, against my will and at considerable cost, the most valuable education I have: an intuition for how institutions actually work as opposed to how they claim to.

Most systems are held together by inertia and good intentions rather than competence and design. The people inside them are frequently capable individually and useless collectively. The gap between the brochure and the reality is where most of life happens. I learned this in waiting rooms and hospital corridors and the offices of specialists charging four hundred dollars to be confidently wrong, and it has proved applicable to nearly everything else.

**The Twenties**

I'll be honest about these, because that's the only version worth writing.

I built an expertise in product and design thinking - the intersection of technology and human behaviour, how people interact with systems, why some products feel inevitable and others feel forced, the thousand small decisions separating something that works from something that merely functions. I gravitated there because I could see, earlier than most people around me, that technology was going to restructure everything. Not in the Silicon Valley poster sense. In the structural, irreversible, civilisational sense - the way electricity restructured everything, the way internal combustion did. I wanted to understand how humans would live on the other side of it.

I was right about the direction and wrong about the timeline. I thought I had decades to position myself and I had years. The acceleration in artificial intelligence over the past twenty-four months has compressed what I expected to be a generational transformation into something happening now, in real time, faster than anyone I know can fully process.

The twenties weren't only work and pain management, and I need to say that, because the illness becomes the whole story if you let it and I've fought hard not to.

I travelled. Forty-five countries before this birthday, and not backpacking in the gap-year sense. Something more deliberate. Each country is a different answer to the same human questions: how do we organise ourselves, what do we build, what do we value, what do we neglect? You can't understand your own country until you've seen enough others to know what's local and what's universal. Australians particularly need this education, because the comfort and isolation of this continent breeds a provincialism that masquerades as worldliness simply because we speak English and watch American television.

I fell in love with Japan, repeatedly, across multiple visits, each one deepening rather than diminishing it. It's difficult to explain because what moves me is the totality rather than any single element of it. A culture treating the making of a bowl of ramen with the seriousness other cultures reserve for constitutional law. A society of 125 million functioning with an efficiency and mutual consideration that would be unrecognisable in Sydney. An aesthetic sensibility so pervasive that a convenience store is better designed than most Australian restaurants. The word kodawari, which translates poorly and means something like an uncompromising commitment to getting things right.

Coming home from Tokyo is an exercise in recalibrating your expectations downward. Japan has almost no natural resources and has built something extraordinary. Australia has almost every natural resource and has built something adequate. That gap is cultural rather than material. It's the difference between a society demanding excellence and one that learned to accept good enough.

**What I Believe at Thirty**

Stated without the diplomatic softening I've learned to apply in conversation.

The next three years will be more consequential than the last thirty. Not for me personally, though they may be that too. For the world, for Australia, for the basic arrangements governing how people live and work.

I believe this because I can see the curves, and the curves don't care about political cycles or institutional comfort.

Solar photovoltaic costs have fallen ninety-nine percent since 1976, and the learning curve is relentless - every doubling of cumulative production cuts costs another twenty to twenty-five percent. We're approaching the point where solar plus storage undercuts the marginal cost of running existing fossil plants, never mind building new ones. When energy becomes effectively free at the margin, everything downstream becomes radically cheaper. Manufacturing. Transport. Computation. Agriculture. Desalination. The entire cost structure of civilisation sits downstream of energy.

Autonomous vehicles work well enough that in controlled environments they already beat human drivers on every safety metric that matters. The technology stopped being the bottleneck some time ago. Liability frameworks are the bottleneck. Insurance models are the bottleneck. Union agreements are the bottleneck. The political influence of industries facing displacement is the bottleneck.

Large language models can already perform the core cognitive tasks of a junior lawyer, a junior accountant, a junior analyst - imperfectly, without seasoned judgement, competently and cheaply enough that the economic logic of hiring a human for those tasks is eroding in real time. Within three years the capabilities will be substantially greater and the displacement undeniable.

Nuclear energy could transform this country's grid inside a decade if we allowed it. Small modular reactors are being deployed elsewhere, the technology is proven, the safety record is extraordinary, and we sit on the largest uranium reserves on earth. We won't do it because four words in legislation prohibit it and no politician has the spine to delete them.

Telemedicine could deliver specialist care to every rural community here. The technology exists and was demonstrated during lockdowns and works. It isn't deployed because regulatory frameworks were written for physical consulting rooms and paper files and nobody with authority has bothered updating them.

Gene therapies for conditions intractable for generations are in advanced trials, with CRISPR-based treatments crawling through approval processes designed for small-molecule drugs in the 1960s. Treatments that could help patients in months take years because the regulatory apparatus can't distinguish between a pill and a technology that edits the human genome. The caution isn't unreasonable in principle and in practice it's killing people through delay.

I could continue. Prefabricated housing. Automated manufacturing. Drone logistics. Desalination at scale. In every case the technology is ready or nearly so, and in every case the bottleneck is identical: regulation written for a world that no longer exists, administered by institutions whose primary function has become self-perpetuation.

**About Australia Specifically**

We've been lucky for so long we've confused luck with competence. The resources were in the ground. The geography was favourable. The American alliance provided security we didn't pay for. Proximity to Asia provided markets we didn't cultivate. We rode all of it for decades and told ourselves it was skill.

The luck is running out, and not because the resources are depleted - we have more lithium, uranium, iron ore, solar exposure and agricultural land than almost any nation on earth, and the raw materials for the next century of civilisation are under our feet and falling on our rooftops. The luck is running out because the institutional capacity to convert those advantages into anything useful is decaying faster than the advantages can compensate.

Now I'm going to say something that will sound inflammatory and that I believe to be straightforwardly true. The combination of regulatory sclerosis and union entrenchment will destroy this country's capacity to navigate what's coming, unless something changes fundamentally and soon.

I say that without ideological attachment. Regulation serves legitimate purposes and unions have played essential historical roles, and I understand the history and respect the achievements. What I observe in 2022 is regulation serving incumbency and unions protecting obsolescence, which is a different thing from regulation serving safety and unions protecting workers.

I've watched it personally. I've sat in planning meetings where a project that should take months was mapped onto years because every conceivable objection had been encoded into process. Environmental assessment. Heritage review. Community consultation. Traffic study. Each individually defensible and collectively paralysing. A process taking months in Tokyo or Seoul takes years here, and not because our buildings are safer or our cities better planned, because they aren't. Because the accumulated weight of procedural requirement has made delay the primary output of the system.

Construction unions operate with an influence that would astonish observers from most developed economies. Work rules preventing adoption of labour-saving technology. Demarcation disputes fragmenting simple tasks into complex multi-trade operations. Enterprise agreements locking in cost structures regardless of productivity. I've watched projects where the technology existed to do the work faster and cheaper and more safely, and the work was done slowly and expensively and dangerously instead, because the agreements required it.

Every dollar of unnecessary construction cost is a dollar on housing prices. Every year of infrastructure delay is a year of foregone benefit. The people who pay are the young Australians who can't afford a house, the communities waiting decades for transport other countries deliver in years, and the businesses that look at the approvals timeline and invest somewhere else.

Energy is worse. Coal is politically toxic, nuclear is legally banned, gas is entangled in moratoriums, renewables face grid connection delays and planning objections stalling projects for years. We're a continent with the most abundant energy resources on earth and some of the highest electricity prices in the developed world. That's policy rather than paradox.

**South Africa**

I think about South Africa more often than I'd like.

The comparison isn't exact, since their trajectory was shaped by a specific and brutal history we don't share. The structural parallel is instructive in ways that should make us uncomfortable.

South Africa had extraordinary natural resources, a functioning industrial base, sophisticated financial institutions, competent infrastructure. Nothing about its current state was destined. What happened was a slow degradation of institutional capacity - state capture, regulatory dysfunction, the progressive hollowing out of the people and processes keeping critical systems running. And, most relevantly, an energy crisis.

Eskom. Load shedding, the euphemism for rolling blackouts that became a feature of daily life. Businesses on generators, hospitals on backup power, an economy haemorrhaging billions annually to unreliability, investment fleeing to jurisdictions where the lights stay on. The descent took two decades of underinvestment and corruption and skills erosion and failure to build new generation while demand grew. The warning signs were visible for years and were ignored, or acknowledged without action, or addressed with policies treating symptoms while deepening causes.

Now imagine Australia in fifteen years on the present course. We've banned our most reliable baseload. We're decommissioning coal on a political timetable bearing no relationship to the readiness of replacements. We're building renewables while grid connection delays leave new capacity idle for years after construction. Battery storage at the required scale doesn't exist on current timelines. Gas, which could bridge the gap, is tangled in export disputes and reservation politics. The Altona refinery closed last year and took downstream industry with it. The aluminium smelters are under constant pressure from energy costs. Manufacturers are watching margins evaporate and starting to ask whether staying is worth it.

When Ford, Holden and Toyota left in 2017 we told ourselves it was the inevitable cost of high wages and a small domestic market. We never asked whether the policy settings that made those departures inevitable were the right settings. We never asked what it meant that a hundred and twenty supplier companies went with them, or what happens to a country that stops making things. We told ourselves services would fill the gap.

They won't. If we don't build new baseload capacity quickly, we face a future where energy scarcity becomes the defining constraint on Australian economic life - in the practical, daily, South African sense. Rolling shortages. Industrial closures. Capital flight. The grinding erosion of living standards we've treated as a birthright.

This is where the regulatory and union problem stops being expensive and becomes existential. Building energy infrastructure requires speed and our system is designed for process, measured in years, challengeable and appealable and relitigable at every stage by anyone with standing and a lawyer.

Mad Max was set in Australia for a reason. George Miller understood something about the fragility of civilisation on a continent this vast and this harsh. Strip away the infrastructure and you're left with distance, heat, and competition for what remains. We're not close to that. We're closer than we were ten years ago, and the direction of travel should concern anyone paying attention.

**Done Modulating**

This is supposed to be a birthday essay and I've spent most of it angry. I notice that and I'm not going to apologise for it.

For most of my twenties I modulated. I softened the edges of what I could see, because unmodulated observation makes people uncomfortable. You sound alarmist when you're being precise. You sound pessimistic when you're being realistic. You learn to present observations in forms people can receive without defensiveness, and in the process you dilute them until they lose their force.

I'm done modulating.

I've spent thirty years learning how the world actually works - through building products, through navigating broken institutions with my own body as the test case, through walking the streets of forty-five countries asking why this place works and that one doesn't. I know what functional systems look like, having experienced them in Tokyo and Singapore and Copenhagen. I know what dysfunctional ones look like, having experienced them in Australian hospitals and planning departments and energy markets.

The next three years will see artificial intelligence transform knowledge work beyond recognition. Energy economics will shift in ways that redistribute geopolitical power. Autonomous systems will move from demonstration to deployment. The gap between countries that adapt and countries that stall will widen into a chasm taking generations to cross.

The technology arriving is a gift rather than a threat. Cheaper energy, smarter tools, longer and healthier lives, problems solved that have resisted solution for generations. All of it within reach, possibly within this decade.

The threat is us. Our institutions. Our regulations. Our unions. Our reflexive preference for the familiar over the functional. A political class that can't understand the internet, let alone what follows it. Our national habit of confusing process with progress and consultation with action.

I'm thirty. I have a condition that won't relent and a mind that won't stop noticing. I've seen enough of the world to know what's possible and enough of Australia to know what we're choosing instead.

The next three years will be more transformative than the last thirty. Everything before was prologue, and the rate of change is about to make the prologue look like it was standing still.

I'm ready for it. I'm not sure this country is. I intend to find out, and I don't intend to be quiet about what I see.
