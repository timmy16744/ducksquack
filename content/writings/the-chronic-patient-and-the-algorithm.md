I've spent fifteen years as a case study in how healthcare systems fail. I offer that as data rather than self-pity. The failures have been consistent enough and repeated enough and documented enough to constitute something like evidence - evidence of what happens when a system optimised for acute intervention meets a condition that never resolves.

Cluster headaches. The literature describes them as among the most painful conditions known to humanity, and in this instance the literature isn't exaggerating. More than half my existence involves managing that pain. The other half involves managing the systems meant to help me manage it, and I'm genuinely unsure which is more exhausting.

Over a hundred medications tried. Six surgical interventions. Procedures that should never have been performed. Diagnoses that were simply wrong. Imaging misread by specialists who were, on paper, experts. Blood tests interpreted incorrectly. Recommendations delivered with confidence that later proved unfounded.

I want to be careful here, because there's a version of this that blames individual clinicians and it would be false. Many of the people I've encountered were skilled and conscientious, doing their best inside constraints they didn't create. The failure is structural. The system was never designed for someone like me.

Modern healthcare excels at acute problems. A heart attack, a broken bone, an infection requiring antibiotics - you'll likely receive competent care, because the protocols exist, the training is thorough, and the feedback loops are tight. The intervention works or it doesn't, and you find out quickly.

Chronic conditions behave differently. They don't resolve, they require management across decades, and they involve uncertainty and experimentation and treatments that help somewhat. The feedback loops are slow and ambiguous. What works today may stop working. What looked like progress may prove temporary.

The system has no idea how to handle any of that. Appointment structures assume discrete problems with discrete solutions. Funding models reward procedures over ongoing care. Specialists see narrow slices of a person and rarely speak to each other. The patient ends up the only continuous thread, the only entity holding the full picture, and the patient's knowledge is systematically discounted for lack of credentials.

I've waited six months to see a specialist. Six months of pain and uncertainty and a life constrained by something that might be better managed if someone with expertise would simply look. The specialist, when finally reached, spends fifteen minutes with a file they've just opened, makes recommendations from incomplete information, and schedules a follow-up in another six months. The cycle continues.

**Where the Machines Could Help**

This is where I start thinking about artificial intelligence. I'm past believing in salvation, so I don't mean it that way. I mean it as a shift in what becomes possible.

Consider diagnosis. These systems analyse imaging in minutes. They cross-reference symptoms against the entire medical literature rather than the subset any individual clinician happens to recall. They identify patterns that human cognition, limited by attention and fatigue and sheer volume, will miss. Diagnostic accuracy already exceeds human performance in several domains, and that's measured rather than speculated.

I think about the MRI studies read incorrectly. The blood work whose significance was missed. The diagnoses that took years when a machine might have reached them in hours. How much of my suffering was extended by the limits of human pattern recognition, and how much could have been avoided with tools that process information at a different scale?

Speed matters as much as accuracy. Six months to see a specialist is a function of scarcity rather than a law of nature - there aren't enough specialists, and their time is allocated by queues that make no distinction between urgent and routine. AI doesn't queue. It has no limited appointment slots. It could provide initial assessment immediately, triaging so that human expertise goes where it's most needed.

I imagine a system where my scans are analysed within minutes of being taken, where anomalies are flagged automatically, where my blood work is interpreted by something considering my full history and the full range of relevant research rather than by a physician glancing at numbers between patients. Where I arrive at an appointment with the preliminary analysis already done, so the precious minutes of human attention go to judgement rather than data gathering.

That isn't fantasy. Every component exists. The barriers are institutional and regulatory and cultural - the deep conservatism of health systems that change slowly even where faster change would save lives.

**What Machines Cannot Do**

I want to complicate the optimism, because the story where AI fixes what humans broke is too simple to be useful.

There's something these systems cannot provide, and I've learned to value it precisely through experiencing its absence. Compassion is close to the right word and not quite it. Presence, perhaps. The quality of being seen as a person rather than a case.

The best care I've received has come overwhelmingly from nurses. In the narrow technical sense they often hold less expertise than the doctors, and the nursing role involves a different kind of attention entirely. A nurse sits with you. A nurse notices you're struggling before you say so. A nurse provides something I can only describe as a maternal presence, the sense that another person cares about your suffering as suffering rather than as a problem to be closed out.

That resists automation, and I don't say so as a mystical claim about human uniqueness. I say it as an observation about what actually helps when you're frightened and vulnerable and in pain. The knowledge that another conscious being is present with you, accompanying you through the experience rather than processing your data, makes a difference no system replicates.

So the failure I've lived through has two components. One is informational: errors of diagnosis, delays in assessment, expertise that is scarce and badly allocated. AI can address that component and address it well. The other is relational: the reduction of patients to cases, the loss of continuity, the absence of anyone actually there. AI can't address that at all, and deployed carelessly it would make it worse, depersonalising a system that already struggles to see people.

The question is whether we can have both - whether these systems can carry the informational burden and free human caregivers to do the relational work only humans can do. Or whether their arrival simply becomes another justification for cutting human contact, optimising metrics while the thing that matters quietly disappears.

If I could set one condition on the deployment, it would be that any efficiency gained from automating diagnostics must be reinvested in clinical contact time rather than returned as a saving. Written into procurement, before the systems arrive.

I'd insist on it because I know exactly how this goes otherwise. Every efficiency in healthcare over my fifteen years has been converted into throughput, and the appointment has gone from twenty minutes to fifteen to twelve. There's no natural force that turns a saved minute into a minute spent with a patient. Somebody has to require it, in advance, in writing, while the technology still looks new enough that the terms are negotiable.

I've learned to build my own infrastructure for survival - the exercise routines, the dietary controls, the careful management of cognitive load and environment. I became my own case manager because the system couldn't manage my case. That taught me self-reliance, which has value, and it also taught me exactly what gets lost when systems fail.

Nobody should have to become an expert in their own condition simply to survive. Nobody should spend years seeking a diagnosis a competent system could deliver in weeks. Nobody should undergo procedures that should never have been performed, recommended by specialists whose incentives pointed away from their welfare.

AI won't fix all of this. Some of it is human, and human failures need human solutions. It could fix some of it, though, and perhaps enough to matter - perhaps enough that the next person with an intractable condition doesn't spend fifteen years learning what I've learned. That seems worth pursuing, even for someone who stopped believing in salvation a long time ago.
