---
title: "The Summer That Came Early"
date: "2019-11-20"
color: "red"
---

The fire season started in September, and that isn't normal. Fire season in southeastern Australia begins, in the pattern most of us grew up with, in December or January. The heat builds, the land dries, the eucalyptus oil hangs in the air like a promise, and eventually a lightning strike or a careless match finishes the sentence. You learn the rhythm as a child. You learn that summer has a dangerous edge and that the beauty of this landscape is inseparable from its capacity for destruction.

September is spring. September is meant to be mild and green, the pause between winter rain and summer heat. In September this year, parts of New South Wales and Queensland were already burning.

By November, writing this, the fires have been going for weeks with no sign of stopping. Sydney - not the bush, not the rural fringe, but a city of five million people - has been under smoke thick enough that air quality exceeded hazardous levels for days at a time. People wore masks to work. Schools kept children inside. The sky turned orange in the middle of the day, not in the distant romantic way bushfire sunsets sometimes colour a horizon, but in the immediate oppressive way that tells you the fire has reached you - that it's in the air you're breathing and the light falling on your face.

I'm in Adelaide, where the smoke has arrived in drifts - less intense than Sydney, and present. You can smell it. That particular Australian smell of eucalyptus ash, which your body registers as danger a moment before your mind catches up.

I want to write about what this feels like, because I think the feeling matters more than the statistics, and the statistics are alarming enough on their own.

It feels like the models have come alive. For years climate science has produced projections - graphs with upward-trending lines, scenarios labelled with bureaucratic precision, conference presentations wrapped in careful language about probabilities and confidence intervals. The projections were always for the future. Always slightly abstract. Always mediated by the professional caution of scientists who understand exactly what overstating a case costs them.

They aren't for the future anymore. The fire season the models projected for 2040 has arrived in 2019, and it has arrived as a pattern rather than as an anomaly anyone could attribute to natural variation. Hotter, drier, earlier, worse. Consistent with the models. Earlier than the median projections suggested and well within the published range, which is the detail nobody mentions, because everyone reads the median and almost nobody reads the range.

This is what it's like when a model stops being a projection and becomes a description. Something shifts in the relationship between the world and what you expect of it. The physical world has been changing gradually for decades. Your expectations change all at once, on a particular afternoon. The assumption that this year will be roughly like last year, that the future extends the past, that the rhythms you grew up inside will hold. That assumption has broken, and it has broken in the air rather than on a page.

Australia is the driest inhabited continent and the most fire-prone. The vegetation evolved to burn, which is a remarkable adaptation in evolutionary terms and a genuinely frightening feature of the landscape you happen to live in. Eucalypts are essentially standing fuel, their oil-rich leaves shaped by millions of years of selection to ignite, burn fast and hot, clear the understory and release seed in the process. The landscape is built to burn, and human settlement inside it depends entirely on the fires staying manageable, the season staying predictable, the patterns holding.

The patterns aren't holding.

What strikes me hardest, watching the political response, is the distance between the scale of the problem and the scale of the conversation about it. The Prime Minister goes on holiday to Hawaii while the fires burn, and I don't think that's merely bad optics. I think it's a genuine failure of comprehension - a failure to see that the fires aren't an event to be managed but a signal to be read. The summer came early because the system that produces summers has changed, the change isn't temporary, and the political vocabulary of response - emergency funding, resource deployment, thoughts and prayers - isn't adequate to the category of thing that's happening.

We're a continent governed by people who think in electoral cycles, confronting a problem that operates on geological ones. The mismatch operates at the level of categories, well below anything policy can reach. Our political system is designed for problems with solutions inside a parliamentary term, and this doesn't have solutions inside a parliamentary term. It has responses, adaptations, investments in resilience that take decades to mature. The system can't see in decades. It can barely see past the next Newspoll.

I'm not a climate scientist and I won't pretend to certainty about what any of this means in detail. But I pay attention to patterns, and the pattern I see is a widening gap between the world that's emerging and the institutions meant to manage it. The fires are the most visible expression of that gap. They aren't the most important one.

The most important one is invisible from any window. It's the slow, compounding failure of a civilisation to update its assumptions against evidence that the assumptions are wrong. We plan for the climate we grew up in. We build for the weather we remember. We govern as though the future will resemble the past, and we do it in a country where the evidence to the contrary is currently drifting through the streets of its largest city.

The summer came early. Whether we're capable of updating is a separate question from whether we're capable of responding, and it's the one that actually decides the next fifty years. I'd like to think we can. What I know is that the smoke reached Adelaide this week, and that a country which needs to smell it before it believes it is a country that will always be arriving late.
